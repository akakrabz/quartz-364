## Problem 1 — True or false (15 pts)

> [!question] Problem 1
> Select True or False for each statement.
>
> **(a)** The backpropagation algorithm involves a forward pass followed by a backwards pass through a computational graph.
>
> **(b)** We have a dataset of $N$ examples with input data $x\in\mathbb R^d$ for linear regression. As $N$ increases and $d$ remains fixed, we will find that applying gradient descent will become more computationally efficient than the closed-form solution.
>
> **(c)** Let $y=\dfrac{x}{\Vert x\Vert_2}$ where $x\in\mathbb R^d$. Then the partial derivative $\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x}\in\mathbb R^d$.
>
> **(d)** The gradient descent algorithm is always guaranteed to converge to a locally optimal point for a minimization problem.
>
> **(e)** We collect a dataset by recording several measurements of weather data each day for one month. We would like to use the data from each day to predict the highest temperature the next day. We may use logistic regression to perform this regression problem.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a) True.** Forward pass computes and stores every node value; backward pass sends adjoints from the seed back to the inputs.
>
> **(b) False.** Closed form costs $O(Nd^2+d^3)$; $k$ steps of gradient descent cost $O(Ndk)$. Both are linear in $N$, so growing $N$ alone does not flip the comparison. Large $d$ is what makes the closed form expensive ([[3-linear-models/computational-cost|computational cost]]).
>
> **(c) False.** $y$ is a *vector* in $\mathbb R^d$, so $\partial y/\partial x$ is a $d\times d$ Jacobian, $\dfrac{1}{\Vert x\Vert_2}\Big(I-\dfrac{xx^\top}{\Vert x\Vert_2^2}\Big)$.
>
> **(d) False.** A step size that is too large oscillates or diverges (HW2 Q1: $\alpha=\tfrac12$ bounces forever, $\alpha=1$ blows up).
>
> **(e) False.** Next-day high temperature is a continuous target: a *regression* problem. Logistic regression is a classifier.

## Problem 2 — `cat`, `stack` and a mask (10 pts)

> [!question] Problem 2
> ```python
> import torch
> a = torch.randint(low=-9, high=9, size=(4, 10))  # tensor of integers
> b = torch.cat((a, a), dim=1)
> c = torch.stack((a, a, a), dim=-1)
> d = ???  # complete this line for part (c)
> e = a[d]
> ```
> **(a)** (3 pts) What will be the shape of tensor `b`?
>
> **(b)** (3 pts) What will be the shape of tensor `c`?
>
> **(c)** (4 pts) We would like tensor `e` to contain all values of `a` that are positive multiples of 5. Fill in the line for `d`.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a) `(4, 20)`.** `cat` joins along an *existing* dimension: $10+10$ columns.
>
> **(b) `(4, 10, 3)`.** `stack` creates a *new* dimension, here the last one, of size 3 (the number of tensors).
>
> **(c)** `d = (a > 0) & (a % 5 == 0)`
>
> Parenthesize both comparisons: `&` binds tighter than `>` and `==`. The values from `randint(-9, 9)` lie in $[-9,8]$, so `e` can only contain 5s. (The key also accepted `a >= 0`; that admits 0, which is a multiple of 5 but not a positive one, see [[6-midterm-1/errata|errata]].)

## Problem 3 — A gradient and two steps (15 pts)

> [!question] Problem 3
> Consider $f(x)=(x^\top x-3)^2$ where $x\in\mathbb R^3$.
>
> **(a)** (6 pts) Determine the gradient $\nabla_xf(x)$.
>
> **(b)** (3 pts) State the minimum value of $f(x)$ and one possible choice of $x$ that minimizes it.
>
> **(c)** (6 pts) Let $x^{(0)}=(1,0,0)$ and the step size $\alpha=\tfrac1{16}$. Apply gradient descent to obtain the iterates at $k=1$ and $k=2$. (If you do not have an answer for part (a), let $\nabla_xf(x)=-x$.)

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a)** Outer chain rule, then $\nabla_x(x^\top x)=2x$:
> $$
> \nabla_xf=2(x^\top x-3)\,\nabla_x(x^\top x-3)=2(x^\top x-3)(2x)=4(x^\top x-3)\,x.
> $$
> **(b)** $f\ge0$ as a square, and $f=0$ exactly when $\Vert x\Vert_2^2=3$. So $\min f=0$, at e.g. $x=(1,1,1)$, $(\sqrt3,0,0)$, $(0,\sqrt3,0)$ or $(0,0,\sqrt3)$ — any point on the sphere of radius $\sqrt3$.
>
> **(c)** $k=1$: $x^{(0)\top}x^{(0)}=1$, so $\nabla f(x^{(0)})=4(1-3)(1,0,0)=(-8,0,0)$ and
> $$
> x^{(1)}=(1,0,0)-\tfrac1{16}(-8,0,0)=\left(\tfrac32,0,0\right).
> $$
> $k=2$: $x^{(1)\top}x^{(1)}=\tfrac94$, so $\nabla f(x^{(1)})=4\left(\tfrac94-3\right)\left(\tfrac32,0,0\right)=-3\left(\tfrac32,0,0\right)=\left(-\tfrac92,0,0\right)$ and
> $$
> x^{(2)}=\left(\tfrac32+\tfrac{9}{32},0,0\right)=\left(\tfrac{57}{32},0,0\right)\approx(1.781,0,0).
> $$
> The iterates head toward $(\sqrt3,0,0)\approx(1.732,0,0)$; the second step slightly overshoots it.
>
> **Fallback** $\nabla f=-x$: $x^{(1)}=(1,0,0)+\tfrac1{16}(1,0,0)=\left(\tfrac{17}{16},0,0\right)$, then $x^{(2)}=\tfrac{17}{16}\cdot\tfrac{17}{16}=\left(\tfrac{289}{256},0,0\right)$.

> [!question] Problem 4
> Consider the computational graph above, where $f(x)=a_6$.
>
> **(a)** (8 pts) Express each node $\{a_i\}_{i=1}^6$ in terms of $x$.
>
> **(b)** (5 pts) Determine the partial derivatives of each successor node with respect to its predecessors, e.g. $\partial a_6/\partial a_5$, $\partial a_6/\partial a_4$, $\partial a_6/\partial a_3$, etc.
>
> **(c)** (12 pts) Determine the adjoints at each node $\bar a_i=\partial f/\partial a_i$.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a)** Each arrow is labelled with the value of the node it *leaves*:
> $$
> a_1=x,\quad a_2=a_1^2=x^2,\quad a_3=e^{a_1}=e^x,\quad a_4=a_1a_3=xe^x,\quad a_5=a_2a_3=x^2e^x,
> $$
> $$
> a_6=a_3+a_4+a_5=x^2e^x+xe^x+e^x.
> $$
> **(b)** Nine arrows, nine local partials (hold the node's other inputs fixed):
>
> | into $a_6$ | into $a_5$ | into $a_4$ | into $a_3$, $a_2$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $\partial a_6/\partial a_5=1$ | $\partial a_5/\partial a_3=a_2$ | $\partial a_4/\partial a_3=a_1$ | $\partial a_3/\partial a_1=e^{a_1}$ |
> | $\partial a_6/\partial a_4=1$ | $\partial a_5/\partial a_2=a_3$ | $\partial a_4/\partial a_1=a_3$ | $\partial a_2/\partial a_1=2a_1$ |
> | $\partial a_6/\partial a_3=1$ | | | |
>
> **(c)** Seed, then right to left. $a_3$ feeds $a_4$, $a_5$ and $a_6$; $a_1$ feeds $a_2$, $a_3$ and $a_4$:
> $$
> \bar a_6=1,\qquad \bar a_5=\frac{\partial a_6}{\partial a_5}\bar a_6=1,\qquad \bar a_4=\frac{\partial a_6}{\partial a_4}\bar a_6=1,
> $$
> $$
> \bar a_3=\frac{\partial a_4}{\partial a_3}\bar a_4+\frac{\partial a_5}{\partial a_3}\bar a_5+\frac{\partial a_6}{\partial a_3}\bar a_6=a_1+a_2+1,\qquad
> \bar a_2=\frac{\partial a_5}{\partial a_2}\bar a_5=a_3,
> $$
> $$
> \bar a_1=\frac{\partial a_2}{\partial a_1}\bar a_2+\frac{\partial a_3}{\partial a_1}\bar a_3+\frac{\partial a_4}{\partial a_1}\bar a_4=2a_1a_3+e^{a_1}(a_1+a_2+1)+a_3.
> $$
> **Check (not asked, but it catches dropped terms):** in $x$, $\bar a_1=2xe^x+e^x(x+x^2+1)+e^x=e^x(x^2+3x+2)$, which is $\frac{d}{dx}\big(x^2e^x+xe^x+e^x\big)$.

## Problem 5 — Linear regression: three judgment calls (15 pts)

> [!question] Problem 5
> All parts are unrelated. Recall that linear regression may be posed as
> $$
> \min_w\frac12\Vert\Phi w-y\Vert_2^2,
> $$
> where $\Phi\in\mathbb R^{N\times d}$, $w\in\mathbb R^d$, $y\in\mathbb R^N$. We may also add L2 regularization for $\lambda>0$: $\min_w\frac12\Vert\Phi w-y\Vert_2^2+\lambda\Vert w\Vert_2^2$.
>
> **(a)** (5 pts) We have data approximating $y_i\approx e^{-w_1x_i}+e^{-w_2x_i}+e^{-w_3x_i}$. For $N\ge3$, can $\{w_i\}_{i=1}^3$ be estimated by linear regression? If so, write $\Phi$ for $N=3$ points. If not, explain.
>
> **(b)** (5 pts) We have data approximating $y_i\approx w_1x_i^2+w_2x_i+w_3\sqrt{x_i}$. Same question.
>
> **(c)** (5 pts) A polynomial was fitted to data with regularization and $\lambda=10$. The fitted curve passes near the points but swings far outside the data in the gaps between them (a deep dip near $x=-1.5$, spikes near the edges). Should $\lambda$ be increased, decreased, or left the same? Briefly explain.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a) No.** Each $w_k$ enters through $e^{-w_kx_i}$, a nonlinear function of the weight. Linear regression needs the model to be *linear in the weights*.
>
> **(b) Yes.** $y_i$ is a linear combination of $w_1,w_2,w_3$; the nonlinear functions of $x$ go into the columns of $\Phi$:
> $$
> \Phi=\begin{bmatrix}x_1^2&x_1&\sqrt{x_1}\\x_2^2&x_2&\sqrt{x_2}\\x_3^2&x_3&\sqrt{x_3}\end{bmatrix}.
> $$
> **(c) Increase $\lambda$.** The fit is overfitted and unstable in every gap between points (e.g. around $x=-1.5$). A larger penalty shrinks the weights and smooths the curve. (Decrease $\lambda$ only if the curve were nearly flat and missed the trend.)

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a)(i)** Predict $+1$ iff $w^\top x\ge0$, so compute the margins $m_i=y_i\,w^\top x_i$; a vector classifies everything when all six are positive.
>
> | $w$ | $w^\top x_1,\dots,w^\top x_6$ | margins $m_1,\dots,m_6$ | all $>0$? | $\prod_i\sigma(m_i)$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | $w_1=(1,1)$ | $-1,-1,-3,2,2,4$ | $-1,-1,-3,-2,-2,-4$ | no | — |
> | $w_2=(-1,-1)$ | $1,1,3,-2,-2,-4$ | $1,1,3,2,2,4$ | **yes** | $0.388$ |
> | $w_3=(0,-2)$ | $2,4,6,-2,-4,-6$ | $2,4,6,2,4,6$ | **yes** | $0.744$ |
> | $w_4=(0,2)$ | $-2,-4,-6,2,4,6$ | $-2,-4,-6,-2,-4,-6$ | no | — |
> | $w_5=(1,-2)$ | $2,5,6,-1,-4,-5$ | $2,5,6,1,4,5$ | **yes** | $0.622$ |
>
> **Answer: $w_2$, $w_3$, $w_5$.**
>
> **(ii) $w_3$.** The likelihood is $\prod_i\sigma(m_i)$ and $\sigma$ is increasing, so bigger margins mean bigger likelihood. Compare the classifying vectors point by point: $w_3$'s margins $(2,4,6,2,4,6)$ beat $w_2$'s $(1,1,3,2,2,4)$ on every point but one tie. Against $w_5$'s $(2,5,6,1,4,5)$, $w_3$ loses only on $x_2$ (4 vs 5, a factor $\sigma(5)/\sigma(4)\approx1.01$) but wins on $x_4$ (2 vs 1, a factor $\sigma(2)/\sigma(1)\approx1.20$) and $x_6$: $w_5$'s margin of only 1 on $(1,1)$ is what sinks it. The products in the table confirm it. (The official key argues from the score table alone; its $w_2$ row lists $w_2^\top x_3=1$, which should be 3 — see [[6-midterm-1/errata|errata]].)
>
> **(b) $W_1$.** Read the representative points off the plot: star $\approx(3,1)$, square $\approx(-3,2)$, triangle $\approx(-2,-3)$. Multiply by each $W$ and take the largest score:
>
> | $W$ | star $(3,1)$ | square $(-3,2)$ | triangle $(-2,-3)$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $W_1$ | $(5,-1,-7)\to1$ ✓ | $(-8,7,4)\to2$ ✓ | $(-1,-4,7)\to3$ ✓ |
> | $W_2$ | $(-1,-7,5)\to3$ ✗ | $(7,4,-8)\to1$ ✗ | $(-4,7,-1)\to2$ ✗ |
> | $W_3$ | $(7,-5,5)\to1$ ✓ | $(-4,8,-8)\to2$ ✓ | $(-7,1,-1)\to2$ ✗ |
> | $W_4$ | $(-5,1,7)\to3$ ✗ | $(8,-7,-4)\to1$ ✗ | $(1,4,-7)\to2$ ✗ |
>
> Shortcut: in one-vs-all each row $w_k$ should point toward its own class's cluster.
