> [!abstract] In one breath
> Least squares is $\min_w\tfrac12\Vert Xw-y\Vert_2^2$; setting the gradient $X^\top(Xw-y)$ to zero gives the **normal equations** $X^\top Xw=X^\top y$. Everything else is a variation: replace $X$ by a feature matrix $\Phi$ (works whenever the model is **linear in the weights**), reweight the rows (substitute $Z=\Sigma X$), or add $\lambda\Vert w\Vert^2$ (ridge: $X^\top X+\lambda I$). Derive, don't memorize — the constants in front change between exams.

## The objective and the normal equations

$$
\min_{w_1,w_0}\ \frac12\sum_{i=1}^N\big(y_i-w_1x_i-w_0\big)^2=\min_w\ \frac12\Vert Xw-y\Vert_2^2,\qquad
X=\begin{bmatrix}x_1&1\\\vdots&\vdots\\x_N&1\end{bmatrix},\quad w=\begin{bmatrix}w_1\\w_0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The lectures also write $\tfrac1N\sum_i(mx_i+b-y_i)^2$. A constant in front changes the gradient's scale, not the minimizer. Squaring treats positive and negative errors the same.

$$
\nabla_w\tfrac12\Vert Xw-y\Vert_2^2=X^\top(Xw-y)=0\;\Longrightarrow\;X^\top Xw=X^\top y\;\Longrightarrow\;\boxed{w^*=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y}
$$

With the $\tfrac1N$ version the gradient is $\tfrac2NX^\top(Xw-y)$, with the same solution.

### Three ways to solve it (Lecture 7)

1. Set each partial derivative to zero and solve the system. Exact.
2. Gradient descent with $\nabla J$. Approximate, cheap per step.
3. Matrix form and the normal equations. Exact, and it generalizes.

The 1-D system, from $\partial J/\partial m=\tfrac2N\sum_ix_i(mx_i+b-y_i)=0$ and $\partial J/\partial b=\tfrac2N\sum_i(mx_i+b-y_i)=0$:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}\sum x_i^2&\sum x_i\\\sum x_i&N\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}m\\b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum x_iy_i\\\sum y_i\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Multiple linear regression with $d$ covariates: $X$ becomes $N\times(d+1)$ with a final column of ones, and every formula stays the same.

## The "linear in the weights" test

Replace $X$ by $\Phi$, whose row $i$ is $\phi(x_i)^\top$; the solution is $w^*=(\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top y$. It works whenever the model is **linear in the unknown weights**. Nonlinear functions of $x$ are fine.

| model | linear regression? | row $i$ of $\Phi$ |
|---|---|---|
| $y\approx w_1x^2+w_2x+w_3\sqrt x$ (Fall 25 5b) | **yes** | $[x_i^2,\ x_i,\ \sqrt{x_i}]$ |
| $y\approx e^{-w_1x}+e^{-w_2x}+e^{-w_3x}$ (Fall 25 5a) | **no**: the weights sit inside the exponentials | — |
| $y\approx A_1\sin\omega_0x+A_2\sin2\omega_0x+A_3\sin3\omega_0x$ (HW3 Q1) | **yes** | $[\sin\omega_0x_i,\ \sin2\omega_0x_i,\ \sin3\omega_0x_i]$ |
| $y\approx w_3x^3+w_2x^2+w_1x+w_0$ (L12) | **yes**; needs $N\ge4$ | $[x_i^3,\ x_i^2,\ x_i,\ 1]$ |
| $y\approx w_1e^x+w_2$ | **yes** | $[e^{x_i},\ 1]$ |
| $y\approx w_1\sin(w_2x)$ | **no** | — |

For $N=3$ in Fall 2025 5(b):
$$
\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}x_1^2&x_1&\sqrt{x_1}\\x_2^2&x_2&\sqrt{x_2}\\x_3^2&x_3&\sqrt{x_3}\end{bmatrix}.
$$
A unique solution needs at least as many points as unknown weights and an invertible $\Phi^\top\Phi$ (independent columns), so HW3 Q1(d) needs $N\ge3$ for the three amplitudes. If $d>N$, $X^\top X$ is $d\times d$ with rank at most $N$: singular, and the plain closed form fails.

## Weighted least squares (Fall 2024 Q5, 35 pts)

$$
\min_{w_1,w_0}\ f=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^N\big[\sigma_i(y_i-w_1x_i-w_0)\big]^2,\qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_1}=-\sum_i\sigma_i^2x_i(y_i-w_1x_i-w_0),\quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial w_0}=-\sum_i\sigma_i^2(y_i-w_1x_i-w_0).
$$
Matrix form $J(w)=\tfrac12\Vert\Sigma(Xw-y)\Vert_2^2$ with $\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_N)$. Substitute $Z=\Sigma X$, $v=\Sigma y$ to get the ordinary problem $\tfrac12\Vert Zw-v\Vert^2$:
$$
\nabla_wJ=Z^\top(Zw-v)=X^\top\Sigma^\top\Sigma Xw-X^\top\Sigma^\top\Sigma y=0\;\Longrightarrow\;w^*=(X^\top\Sigma^\top\Sigma X)^{-1}X^\top\Sigma^\top\Sigma y.
$$
With every $\sigma_i=1$, $\Sigma=I$ and this is ordinary least squares. The substitution trick turns any reweighted least-squares problem into the standard one. Full solution: [[6-midterm-1/fall-2024#problem-5--weighted-linear-regression-35-pts|Fall 2024 Q5]].

## Ridge regression (L2 regularization)

$$
\min_w\ \Vert Xw-y\Vert_2^2+\lambda\Vert w\Vert_2^2:\quad\nabla=2X^\top(Xw-y)+2\lambda w=0\;\Longrightarrow\;w^*=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y.
$$

> [!trap] Scaling matters
> With $\tfrac12$ on the data term only — $\tfrac12\Vert\Phi w-y\Vert^2+\lambda\Vert w\Vert^2$, as written in Fall 2025 — the solution is $(\Phi^\top\Phi+2\lambda I)^{-1}\Phi^\top y$. Derive it from the gradient instead of memorizing.

- Larger $\lambda$: smaller weights, smoother curve, risk of **underfitting** (a flat line).
- Smaller $\lambda$: closer to plain least squares, risk of **overfitting** (wild swings between data points).
- $\lambda>0$ makes $X^\top X+\lambda I$ invertible even when $d>N$, and it reduces the pull of outliers (Lecture 8).

| what the fitted curve does | answer |
|---|---|
| swings far outside the data between points, spikes near the edges (Fall 25 5c at $\lambda=10$; HW3 2b) | **increase** $\lambda$: overfit |
| nearly flat, misses the obvious trend (HW3 2c) | **decrease** $\lambda$: underfit |
| smooth and follows the trend through the noise (HW3 2a) | leave $\lambda$ |

## Cost: closed form vs gradient descent (Lecture 8)

| method | cost |
|---|---|
| $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ | $O(Nd^2+d^3)$: forming $X^\top X$ is $Nd^2$, inverting is $d^3$ |
| gradient descent | $O(Nd)$ per iteration, $O(Ndk)$ for $k$ iterations |

When $N\gg d$ both are linear in $N$, so growing $N$ alone does not make gradient descent the cheaper option ([[6-midterm-1/fall-2025#problem-1--true-or-false-15-pts|Fall 2025 T/F b]] is False). Large $d$ is what makes the closed form expensive.

## Why not use linear regression for classification? (Lecture 8)

Predicting $\hat y=\operatorname{sgn}(mx+b)$ with $\pm1$ labels works for balanced, well-separated data. It breaks when one class has extra or far-away points, which drag the least-squares line and misclassify data that is still linearly separable, and for more than two classes, where labels 0, 1, 2 impose a meaningless order. [[3-linear-models/logistic-regression|Logistic regression]] fixes both.
