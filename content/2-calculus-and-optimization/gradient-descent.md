*Lecture 4 · Homework 2 Q1 · [[2-calculus-and-optimization/index|Calculus and optimization]] · builds on [[2-calculus-and-optimization/gradients-and-partial-derivatives|gradients]] and [[2-calculus-and-optimization/primal-optimization|primal optimization]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Instead of solving $\nabla f=0$, start from a guess and repeatedly step against the gradient: $x^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}-\alpha\nabla f(x^{(k)})$. The **step size** (learning rate) $\alpha>0$ decides everything: too small is slow, too large oscillates or diverges. On a convex function a suitable $\alpha$ reaches the global minimum; on a non-convex one you land in whichever local minimum is downhill from the start.

## What it is

The gradient points in the direction of steepest **ascent** ([[2-calculus-and-optimization/gradients-and-partial-derivatives|gradients]]), so a small step along $-\nabla f$ lowers $f$. Gradient descent turns this into an algorithm.

> [!definition] Definition — gradient descent
> Pick an initial guess $x^{(0)}$ and a step size $\alpha>0$, then iterate
> $$
> x^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}-\alpha\,\nabla f\bigl(x^{(k)}\bigr),\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots
> $$
> until the gradient is (close to) zero or a fixed number of iterations is reached. With several parameters $\theta=(a,b,c,\ldots)$, every coordinate is updated at once with the same $\alpha$: $\theta^{(k+1)}=\theta^{(k)}-\alpha\nabla_\theta f(\theta^{(k)})$. For maximization, **gradient ascent** uses $+\alpha\nabla f$.

The method only needs to **evaluate** $\nabla f$ at the current point — exactly what [[2-calculus-and-optimization/backpropagation|backpropagation]] provides — so it scales to millions of parameters.

## What you should know

- The update equation, and that the gradient is evaluated at the **current** iterate $x^{(k)}$.
- How to do two iterations by hand, in exact fractions, for a function of 1–3 variables.
- **Step-size behaviour** on a quadratic: the error is multiplied by $1-\alpha f''$ each step, so GD converges iff $0<\alpha<2/f''$ — monotone if $\alpha<1/f''$, in one step if $\alpha=1/f''$, zig-zagging if $1/f''<\alpha<2/f''$, oscillating forever at $2/f''$, diverging beyond.
- GD is **not** guaranteed to converge (too large $\alpha$), and is guaranteed to find the **global** minimum only for convex functions (with a suitable $\alpha$).
- At a minimum $\nabla f=0$, so the updates stop by themselves; the gradient's magnitude is a natural stopping criterion.
- The same loop with autograd gradients is how every model in the course is trained ([[2-calculus-and-optimization/fitting-models-with-gradient-descent|fitting models]], then [[4-pytorch-training/optimizers|`torch.optim.SGD`]]).

## Worked: one variable

Lecture 4: minimize $f(x)=(x-1)^2$ from $x^{(0)}=4$ with $\alpha=\tfrac14$. The gradient is $\nabla f(x)=2x-2$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{(1)}&=4-\tfrac14(2\cdot4-2)=4-\tfrac32=\tfrac52,\\
x^{(2)}&=\tfrac52-\tfrac14\bigl(2\cdot\tfrac52-2\bigr)=\tfrac52-\tfrac34=\tfrac74,\\
x^{(3)}&=\tfrac74-\tfrac14\bigl(2\cdot\tfrac74-2\bigr)=\tfrac74-\tfrac38=\tfrac{11}{8},\qquad x^{(4)}=\tfrac{19}{16},\ \ldots\to1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Each step halves the distance to the minimizer: $x^{(k+1)}-1=(1-2\alpha)(x^{(k)}-1)=\tfrac12(x^{(k)}-1)$. The lecture's loop:

```python
x, alpha = 4, 1/4
x_values = [x]
for n in range(30):
    gradient = 2*x - 2          # f'(x) at current x
    x = x - alpha*gradient      # gradient descent step
    x_values.append(x)
```

```
[4, 2.5, 1.75, 1.375, 1.1875, 1.09375, 1.046875, ...]
```

## The step size decides everything

Homework 2 Q1: $f(x)=2x^2-6x+5$, $f'(x)=4x-6$, minimizer $x^*=\tfrac32$, start $x^{(0)}=0$.

| $\alpha$ | $x^{(1)},\,x^{(2)},\,x^{(3)}$ | factor $1-4\alpha$ | behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1/10$ | $3/5,\ 24/25,\ 147/125$ | $0.6$ | monotone, slow |
| $1/4$ | $3/2,\ 3/2,\ 3/2$ | $0$ | lands on $x^*$ in one step |
| $1/3$ | $2,\ 4/3,\ 14/9$ | $-1/3$ | zig-zags inward, converges |
| $1/2$ | $3,\ 0,\ 3$ | $-1$ | bounces between 0 and 3 forever |
| $1$ | $6,\ -12,\ 42$ | $-3$ | diverges |

> [!derivation]- Why the factor is $1-\alpha f''$
> For a quadratic, $f'(x)=f''\cdot(x-x^*)$ with constant $f''$. Then
> $$
> x^{(k+1)}-x^*=x^{(k)}-x^*-\alpha f''(x^{(k)}-x^*)=(1-\alpha f'')(x^{(k)}-x^*),
> $$
> so the error after $k$ steps is $(1-\alpha f'')^k(x^{(0)}-x^*)$. It shrinks iff $\lvert1-\alpha f''\rvert<1$, i.e. $0<\alpha<2/f''$. Here $f''=4$: converge iff $0<\alpha<\tfrac12$. For non-quadratic functions the same holds near a minimum, with $f''$ evaluated there.

## Worked: several variables (the exam's two-step problem)

> [!recipe] Recipe — two steps of gradient descent by hand
> 1. Write $\nabla f$ symbolically (often a separate part worth ~6 points).
> 2. Evaluate it at the **current** iterate $x^{(k)}$ — every coordinate from the old point, none from the new one.
> 3. Update every coordinate: $x^{(k+1)}=x^{(k)}-\alpha\nabla f(x^{(k)})$. Keep exact fractions (or $e$).
> 4. Repeat for $k=1$; box both iterates.
> 5. If the numbers turn ugly, look for a slip — exams choose $\alpha$ so the arithmetic collapses. If you could not do step 1, use the fallback gradient the question gives.

**Fall 2024 Q3.** $f(x,y)=(x+3y)^2+(x-2)^2$, start $(0,0)$, $\alpha=\tfrac18$.

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2(x+3y)+2(x-2),\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=6(x+3y).
$$

$$
\nabla f(0,0)=(-4,\,0)\ \Rightarrow\ (x,y)^{(1)}=(0,0)-\tfrac18(-4,0)=\bigl(\tfrac12,\,0\bigr),
$$

$$
\nabla f\bigl(\tfrac12,0\bigr)=\bigl(1-3,\ 3\bigr)=(-2,\,3)\ \Rightarrow\ (x,y)^{(2)}=\bigl(\tfrac12+\tfrac14,\ 0-\tfrac38\bigr)=\bigl(\tfrac34,\,-\tfrac38\bigr).
$$

**Fall 2025 Q3.** $f(x)=(x^\top x-3)^2$, $x\in\mathbb{R}^3$, $x^{(0)}=(1,0,0)$, $\alpha=\tfrac1{16}$. By the chain rule and $\nabla_x x^\top x=2x$ ([[2-calculus-and-optimization/matrix-calculus|matrix calculus]]): $\nabla f=2(x^\top x-3)\cdot2x=4(x^\top x-3)x$.

$$
\nabla f(x^{(0)})=4(1-3)(1,0,0)=(-8,0,0)\ \Rightarrow\ x^{(1)}=(1,0,0)+\tfrac{8}{16}(1,0,0)=\bigl(\tfrac32,0,0\bigr),
$$

$$
x^{(1)\top}x^{(1)}=\tfrac94,\quad \nabla f(x^{(1)})=4\bigl(\tfrac94-3\bigr)\bigl(\tfrac32,0,0\bigr)=\bigl(-\tfrac92,0,0\bigr)\ \Rightarrow\ x^{(2)}=\bigl(\tfrac32+\tfrac{9}{32},0,0\bigr)=\bigl(\tfrac{57}{32},0,0\bigr).
$$

The iterates move toward the sphere $\Vert x\Vert_2=\sqrt3\approx1.732$ where $f=0$ ($57/32\approx1.781$ has slightly overshot it).

The figure shows a fact worth knowing even though no exam asked: with several variables the step must satisfy $\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}$, where $\lambda_{\max}$ is the largest curvature (Hessian eigenvalue). Fall 2024's $\alpha=\tfrac18$ is above $2/20.2$, so its two steps are right but a third, fourth, … step would run away.

> [!question]- Practice: two steps on $f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2-2x$ from $(0,1)$ with $\alpha=\tfrac14$
> $\nabla f=(2x-2,\ 4y)$. $\nabla f(0,1)=(-2,4)\Rightarrow(0,1)-\tfrac14(-2,4)=\bigl(\tfrac12,0\bigr)$. $\nabla f(\tfrac12,0)=(-1,0)\Rightarrow\bigl(\tfrac34,0\bigr)$. The minimizer is $(1,0)$: the $y$-coordinate got there in one step because $\alpha=1/f_{yy}=1/4$, while $x$ halves its distance each step ($1-\alpha f_{xx}=\tfrac12$).

## A non-quadratic example

Lecture 4's exercise applies GD to the function whose critical point cannot be found by hand ([[2-calculus-and-optimization/primal-optimization#why-not-always-set-the-gradient-to-zero|primal optimization]]):

$$
f(x)=\tfrac13(x-1)^6-(x-2)^5+5x^4,\qquad f'(x)=2(x-1)^5-5(x-2)^4+20x^3 .
$$

From $x^{(0)}=0$, $f'(0)=-2-80=-82$, so the first step moves right by $82\alpha$.

```python
x, alpha = 0, 1e-3
for n in range(100):
    gradient = 2*(x-1)**5 - 5*(x-2)**4 + 20*x**3
    x = x - alpha*gradient
```

| $\alpha$ | $x^{(1)}$ | $x^{(100)}$ | behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10^{-4}$ | $0.0082$ | $0.461$ | correct direction, far too slow |
| $10^{-3}$ | $0.082$ | $0.8014$ | nearly at $x^*\approx0.8018$ |
| $10^{-2}$ | $0.82$ | $0.8018$ | converged within a few steps |
| $2\cdot10^{-2}$ | $1.64$ | diverges | overshoots into a steeper region and blows up |

(Values computed by running the loop. The exercise text asks for $\alpha=10^{-3}$, but the notebook's printed run, `0, 0.0082, 0.0163, …`, used `alpha = 1e-4`: the first row. The minimum has $f''(x^*)\approx73$, so near it the safe range is $\alpha<2/73\approx0.027$, and $\alpha=0.02$ fails only because its first step lands where $f$ is much steeper.)

With $\alpha=\tfrac1{20}$, starting at 4 ends at 2.57, starting at 2 (or 1) ends at 0.86, starting at 0 finds −0.95. With $x^{(0)}=0$ and $\alpha=\tfrac14$ the iterates never settle and keep jumping between basins. The two failure modes of gradient descent are therefore:

1. **Not convex →** no guarantee of the global minimum ([[2-calculus-and-optimization/primal-optimization#convexity|convexity]]).
2. **Step too large →** oscillation or divergence; step too small → painfully slow.

Later lectures add tools for these: momentum, learning-rate schedules and adaptive optimizers ([[4-pytorch-training/optimizers|optimizers]], [[4-pytorch-training/learning-rate-schedulers|schedulers]]).

> [!trap] Common mistakes
> - Using an already-updated coordinate to compute the gradient for the next coordinate. Compute the whole gradient at $x^{(k)}$ first.
> - Adding instead of subtracting: descent is $x-\alpha\nabla f$.
> - "Gradient descent always converges to a local minimum" — **False** (Fall 2025 T/F): too large an $\alpha$ oscillates or diverges.
> - Decimals instead of fractions; one rounding slip in step 1 ruins step 2.
