*Lecture 19 · [[7-transformers/index|Transformers]] · builds on [[5-deep-networks/lstm-and-gru|LSTM and GRU]], [[5-deep-networks/bidirectional-and-stacked-rnns|bidirectional RNNs]] and [[7-transformers/tokenization|tokenization]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> **Sequence transduction** maps an input sequence to an output sequence of a possibly different length; translation is the standard example. A **sequence-to-sequence (S2S) RNN** reads the source sentence with an **encoder** RNN, keeps only its final hidden state — the **context vector** — and hands it to a **decoder** RNN as its initial state; the decoder then emits one target word per step, starting from `<SOS>` and stopping at `<EOS>`. **Teacher forcing** feeds the decoder the true previous word during training instead of its own guess. Translations are scored with **BLEU**, a 0–1 measure of matching 1- to 4-word groups. On the lecture's English→French data the RNNs fit the training set but reach a validation BLEU of only about 0.27–0.37, and three structural limits — sequential computation, everything squeezed into hidden states, one context vector between encoder and decoder — are what the [[7-transformers/attention|attention]]-based Transformer removes.

## What it is

Every RNN task so far produced one label per sequence ([[5-deep-networks/recurrent-neural-networks|surname → language]], [[7-transformers/tokenization|review title → sentiment]]). Translation needs a whole output sequence, and "Il nous faut discuter de quelque chose de très important." has a different word order from "We have something very important that we need to discuss." (both happen to be 11 tokens; in general the lengths differ). Lecture 19 builds the classic answer from two RNNs.

> [!definition] Definitions
> - **Sequence transduction**: converting an input sequence $x = (x_1,\dots,x_{d_{in}})$ into an output sequence $y = (y_1,\dots,y_{d_{out}})$, where $d_{in}$ need not equal $d_{out}$.
> - **Encoder** $f$: an RNN that reads $x$ and returns its last hidden state $h_{enc} = f(x)\in\mathbb R^{H}$, the **context vector**.
> - **Decoder** $g$: an RNN started from $h_{-1} = h_{enc}$ and $w_{-1} = $ `<SOS>`, producing scores over the target vocabulary at each step: $\hat y_t = g(w_{t-1}, h_{t-1})$, where $w_{t-1}$ is the previous word.
> - **Teacher forcing**: during training, $w_{t-1}$ is the **ground-truth** previous target word (the target shifted right by one, starting with `<SOS>`) instead of the model's own previous prediction.

## What you should know

- The encoder–decoder structure, what the context vector is, and that the decoder runs one step per output word until it predicts `<EOS>` (or hits `MAX_LENGTH = 25`).
- The special tokens: `<SOS>` = 0 starts decoding, `<EOS>` = 1 ends every sentence; source and target languages have **separate** vocabularies and embeddings.
- Teacher forcing: what is fed in, why it speeds up and stabilizes training, and why it can only be used in training (at inference there is no target).
- Greedy decoding: at every step take the arg-max word and feed it back.
- How the loss compares a predicted sequence and a target of different lengths (the notebook pads the target with `<EOS>` or trims it).
- BLEU: clipped $n$-gram precisions for $n = 1..4$, their geometric mean, and the brevity penalty; why short or reordered translations can score 0.
- The lecture's conclusions from the four saved runs, and the **three limitations** of S2S RNNs, each paired with how the Transformer fixes it.

## The English→French data

The course provides `eng-fra.txt` (135,842 tab-separated sentence pairs) and an `English2FrenchDataset` class in `helpers.py`.

![[l19-seq2seq-rnn.png|Encoder reads "le chat est noir <EOS>", passes a context vector to the decoder, which outputs "the cat is black <EOS>" one token at a time]]

The lecture's diagram (from the PyTorch translation tutorial; it shows French→English) has every ingredient: token indices, an encoder, a single **context** arrow, and a decoder whose input row is the target shifted right behind `<SOS>` — teacher forcing — and whose output row ends in `<EOS>`.

**Tokenization.** Each sentence is split on spaces and trailing punctuation is split off (`"discuss."` → `discuss`, `.`), as in [[7-transformers/tokenization|Lecture 18]], but case is kept. A word belongs to a vocabulary only if it occurs at least `min_freq` times in that language, and a pair is kept only if **every** word on **both** sides is frequent. Each vocabulary starts with `<SOS>` = 0 and `<EOS>` = 1, and every stored sentence ends in `<EOS>`:

```python
def build_dictionaries(self, freq_list, is_target=False):
    word_to_idx, idx_to_word = {'<SOS>': 0, '<EOS>': 1}, {0: '<SOS>', 1: '<EOS>'}
    idx_ctr = 2
    for w in freq_list:
        word_to_idx[w] = idx_ctr
        idx_to_word[idx_ctr] = w
        idx_ctr += 1
    return word_to_idx, idx_to_word
```

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 50 | 37,756 | 1,443 | 1,646 | first look; Lecture 20's transformers |
| 200 | 9,976 (4,988 train / 4,988 validation) | 542 | 560 | Lecture 19's models |

Each sentence tensor has shape $(L, 1)$: $L$ time steps, batch of one. Both sentences end in index 1, `<EOS>`; `de` appears twice in the French (index 39).

**Several correct answers.** The same English sentence often appears with different French translations: "I left ." with "Je suis parti ." and "Je suis partie ."; "We have to go ." with "Il nous faut partir ." and "Il nous faut y aller ."; "I don't know what I'm going to do with you ." with 12 versions (*avec vous* / *de vous*, *ne sais* / *ne sais pas* / *J'ignore*, …). `build_reference_dictionary` collects every French sentence paired with a given English sentence, and BLEU scores a translation against **all** of them. At `min_freq = 200`, 43% of pairs share their English side with another pair (7,528 distinct English sentences among 9,976 pairs).

## The model

Both halves are single-layer [`nn.GRU`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.GRU.html)s with their own [`nn.Embedding`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.Embedding.html) (embedding and hidden size 64). The encoder returns only its final hidden state; if it is bidirectional, the forward and backward final states are concatenated, so the decoder's hidden size becomes $2H$.

```python
class EncoderRNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, num_embeddings, embedding_dim, hidden_dim, n_layers, bidirectional):
        super(EncoderRNN, self).__init__()
        self.n_layers, self.hidden_dim = n_layers, hidden_dim
        self.embedding = nn.Embedding(num_embeddings, embedding_dim)
        self.rnn = nn.GRU(embedding_dim, hidden_dim, n_layers,
                          bidirectional=bidirectional, batch_first=False)
        self.D = 2 if bidirectional else 1

    def forward(self, x):
        h0 = torch.zeros(self.D*self.n_layers, x.size(1), self.hidden_dim).to(DEVICE)
        z = self.embedding(x)
        outputs, hiddens = self.rnn(z, h0)
        if self.D == 2:   # join forward and backward states
            hiddens = hiddens.view(self.n_layers, self.D, self.hidden_dim)
            return torch.cat((hiddens[:, 0], hiddens[:, 1]), dim=1).unsqueeze(1)
        return hiddens    # (n_layers, 1, hidden_dim): the context vector
```

The decoder runs one GRU step at a time, `MAX_LENGTH = 25` steps at most, and stops as soon as its arg-max word is `<EOS>`:

```python
def forward(self, h_enc, target_tensor=None, teacher_forcing=False):
    decoder_input = torch.tensor([[0]]).long()   # begin with <SOS>
    decoder_hidden = h_enc                       # context vector
    output_predictions = []
    for i in range(MAX_LENGTH):
        decoder_input = decoder_input.to(DEVICE)
        output_prediction, decoder_hidden = self.forward_step(decoder_input, decoder_hidden)
        output_predictions.append(output_prediction)
        if teacher_forcing and i < len(target_tensor):
            decoder_input = torch.tensor([target_tensor[i]]).unsqueeze(1)   # true word
        else:
            _, decoder_input = output_prediction.topk(1)                    # own guess
            decoder_input = decoder_input.squeeze(1).detach()
        _, predicted_token = output_prediction.topk(1)
        if (predicted_token.view(-1)[0]).item() == 1:   # predicted <EOS>
            break
    return torch.cat(output_predictions, dim=0).squeeze(1)   # (n_words, |V_dec|)

def forward_step(self, x, h):
    z = self.embedding(x)
    outputs, hiddens = self.rnn(z, h)
    return self.fc(outputs), hiddens     # scores over the French vocabulary
```

[`Tensor.topk(1)`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.Tensor.topk.html) returns the best score and its index; the index is the next input word, [`detach`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.Tensor.detach.html)ed because a word choice is not differentiable anyway.

### Teacher forcing, and lining up the loss

Without teacher forcing, a wrong early word ("was" instead of "is") becomes the next input, so later predictions are trained on inputs that should never have occurred: gradients are noisy and learning is slow at the start. With teacher forcing every step sees the correct previous word. It is a **training-only** device — at inference the decoder must feed back its own predictions, so the model is used in a slightly different regime from the one it was trained in.

Because the decoder may stop early or run long, the prediction and the target can have different lengths. `translation_train` makes them match before [`nn.CrossEntropyLoss`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss.html):

```python
if len(predictions) > len(french_tensor):          # too long: pad target with <EOS>
    eos_value = dataset.french_word_to_idx['<EOS>']
    pad_amount = len(predictions) - len(french_tensor)
    french_tensor = nn.functional.pad(french_tensor, (0, pad_amount), mode='constant', value=eos_value)
else:                                              # too short: trim target
    french_tensor = french_tensor[:len(predictions)]
example_loss = criterion(predictions, french_tensor)/len(batch)
```

A model that does not stop is therefore penalized at every extra step for not saying `<EOS>`. Gradients of 64 sentences are summed (each loss divided by the batch size) before one SGD step (`lr=1e-2`, `momentum=0.99`); see [[4-pytorch-training/training-loop|the training loop]].

## BLEU: scoring a translation

Accuracy does not work for translation: "Il faut que nous discutions…" is a fine translation that matches almost no word **position** of "Il nous faut discuter…". The **BLEU** score ([Papineni et al., 2002](https://aclanthology.org/P02-1040/)) instead counts shared word groups.

> [!definition] BLEU (sentence level, as computed here)
> For $n = 1,2,3,4$, the **clipped $n$-gram precision** $p_n$ is the number of $n$-grams of the candidate that also occur in a reference — each counted at most as often as it occurs in the reference that contains it most — divided by the number of $n$-grams in the candidate. With candidate length $c$ and reference length $r$ (the reference closest in length),
> $$
> \text{BLEU} = \text{BP}\cdot\Big(\prod_{n=1}^{4} p_n\Big)^{1/4},\qquad \text{BP} = \begin{cases} 1 & c > r\\ e^{\,1 - r/c} & c\le r.\end{cases}
> $$
> The **brevity penalty** BP stops a model from scoring well by outputting only a few safe words. Without smoothing, if any $p_n = 0$ the score is 0.

The notebook uses torchmetrics' [`SacreBLEUScore`](https://lightning.ai/docs/torchmetrics/stable/text/sacre_bleu_score.html) with its defaults (`n_gram=4`, `smooth=False`, case-sensitive), which returns the score as a number between 0 and 1, not 0–100. The lecture's rule of thumb, with [Google's guide](https://docs.cloud.google.com/translate/docs/bleu-scores): scores above 0.30 are already reasonably good translations.

**Worked examples** against the single reference "Je pense que Tom est fatigué ." (7 tokens):

| Candidate | $p_1$ | $p_2$ | $p_3$ | $p_4$ | BP | BLEU |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Je pense que Tom est fatigué . | 7/7 | 6/6 | 5/5 | 4/4 | 1 | **1.000** |
| Je pense que Tom est malade . | 6/7 | 4/6 | 3/5 | 2/4 | 1 | **0.643** |
| Je pense que Tom . | 5/5 | 3/4 | 2/3 | 1/2 | $e^{1-7/5} = 0.670$ | **0.474** |
| Tom est fatigué je pense . | 5/6 | 2/5 | 1/4 | 0/3 | — | **0** |

Row 2: $(6/7\cdot 4/6\cdot 3/5\cdot 2/4)^{1/4} = 0.1714^{1/4} = 0.643$. Row 3 has perfect-looking precisions but is cut short: $0.25^{1/4}\cdot 0.670 = 0.707\cdot0.670 = 0.474$. Row 4 uses five of the right words, but no 4-word group matches (and `je` ≠ `Je`), so without smoothing the score collapses to 0. For the notebook's own example, "Il faut que nous discutions de quelque chose de très important ." against "Il nous faut discuter de quelque chose de très important ." scores $p = (10/12, 6/11, 5/10, 4/9)$ and BLEU $= 0.564$.

**Clipping** stops repetition from paying: "le le le le" against "le chat est noir" has $p_1 = 1/4$, not $4/4$, because `le` occurs only once in the reference.

The lecture's discussion answers:

- **Training:** bidirectionality helps with or without teacher forcing. Teacher forcing speeds up training — more stable guidance and less noisy gradients early on; without it the model's random early guesses become its inputs. (At evaluation step 20 the training BLEU is 0.33 / 0.45 unidirectional without / with forcing and 0.80 / 0.96 bidirectional.)
- **Validation:** bidirectionality is better either way; teacher forcing does not change the converged validation performance much.

Every model fits the training sentences far better than the validation sentences (0.82–0.99 against 0.27–0.37): 4,988 sentence pairs are a small corpus, and the gap is [[4-pytorch-training/experimental-setup|overfitting]].

| Limitation | Why | Transformer fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. **Computation cannot be parallelized.** | Step $t$ needs the hidden state of step $t-1$ — even with teacher forcing — so every sentence is processed word by word in training too. | [[7-transformers/attention#masked-self-attention\|Masked self-attention]] computes all next-word predictions of a teacher-forced sentence at once. |
| 2. **Relationships between words are packed into hidden states.** | Whatever the network knows about earlier words must survive in one vector updated at every step; distant words are easily forgotten (and gradients vanish over long spans). | [[7-transformers/attention\|Self-attention]] connects every pair of tokens directly. |
| 3. **Encoder and decoder interact through one context vector.** | The decoder gets the whole source sentence as one $H$-dimensional vector, and overwrites it as it decodes. | [[7-transformers/attention#cross-attention\|Cross-attention]] lets every decoder position read every encoder output, at every step. |

> [!note] Beyond the lecture: where these models came from
> The encoder–decoder RNN is from [Sutskever, Vinyals and Le (2014)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.3215), with 4-layer LSTMs, 34.8 BLEU (on the usual 0–100 scale) on WMT'14 English→French, and the trick of reversing the source sentence. [Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.0473) argued that the fixed-length context vector is the bottleneck and let the decoder attend over all encoder states — limitation 3 — while keeping the RNNs. [[7-transformers/attention-is-all-you-need|"Attention Is All You Need"]] then removed the recurrence altogether.

> [!trap] Common mistakes and notebook quirks
> - **Teacher forcing at test time.** It needs the true translation, so it is impossible at inference; evaluation (`evaluate_model`) always decodes from the model's own predictions.
> - **Globals instead of attributes.** `Seq2SeqRNN.forward` calls `encoder(x)` and `decoder(...)` — the notebook's global variables — not `self.encoder` and `self.decoder`. It works only because those globals hold the same objects; a second model built in the same notebook would silently use the first one's parts.
> - **Early stop also under teacher forcing.** The decoder loop breaks as soon as its arg-max is `<EOS>`, even when teacher forcing; the target is then trimmed to the shorter prediction, so the remaining target words get no loss in that example.
> - **BLEU of short sentences is 0.** With `smooth=False`, a candidate with fewer than four tokens has no 4-grams, so a perfect "Je comprends ." scores 0; 72 of the 4,988 validation sentences have three French tokens or fewer. The curves average **sentence** BLEU over a fresh random 1,000 sentences at every evaluation, so they are noisy and not the corpus-level BLEU papers report.
> - **Validation overlaps training.** The split is over pairs, and 25.6% of validation English sentences also occur in the training split (with another French translation); the reference dictionary spans both splits, so a memorized training translation can earn validation credit.
> - **Different vocabularies.** `<SOS>`/`<EOS>` are 0/1 in both languages, but every other index means a different word on each side; the decoder's output size is the **French** vocabulary.

> 1. During training with teacher forcing, what is the decoder's input at step 3 for the target "Je suis parti . `<EOS>`"? And at inference?
> 2. "I left ." has the references "Je suis parti ." and "Je suis partie .". Compute BLEU for the candidate "Je suis parti .".
> 3. Why can a bidirectional encoder not be used for the **decoder**?
> 4. Which of the three limitations does teacher forcing alone fix?
>
> > [!solution]- Answers
> > 1. Steps 1, 2, 3 receive `<SOS>`, `Je`, `suis`, so step 3's input is `suis` — the true second word. At inference it is whatever the model predicted at step 2.
> > 2. 1.000: every $n$-gram matches the first reference ($p_n = 4/4, 3/3, 2/2, 1/1$) and the closest reference has the same length, so BP = 1.
> > 3. The decoder generates left to right; reading the output backwards would require words it has not produced yet.
> > 4. None. It stabilizes and speeds up training, but the decoder still runs step by step, still keeps everything in its hidden state, and still sees the source only through the context vector.
