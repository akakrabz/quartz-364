*Paper · Lectures 19–20 · [[7-transformers/index|Transformers]] · Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser and Polosukhin (Google Brain and Google Research), 2017 · [arXiv:1706.03762](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> In 2017 the best translation systems were recurrent (or convolutional) encoder–decoders joined by attention. This paper drops the recurrence and the convolutions and keeps **only attention**, plus position-wise feed-forward layers, residual connections, layer normalization and positional encodings. The result, the **Transformer**, is far more parallel to train and better: 28.4 BLEU on WMT 2014 English→German and 41.8 on English→French, trained in 3.5 days on eight GPUs (the smaller base model needed 12 hours). Lectures 19 and 20 rebuild this model piece by piece; this page reads the paper itself.

## What you should know

- The claim: attention alone, without recurrence, gives better translations at a fraction of the training cost, because every position can be computed in parallel and every pair of positions is one step apart.
- The base model's numbers: $N = 6$ encoder and 6 decoder layers, $d_{\text{model}} = 512$, $h = 8$ heads with $d_k = d_v = 64$, feed-forward width $d_{ff} = 2048$, about 65 million parameters.
- Equation 1, $\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V$, and the reason for $\sqrt{d_k}$: dot products of random unit-variance vectors have variance $d_k$, which pushes the softmax into saturation.
- Multi-head attention with $d_k = d_v = d_{\text{model}}/h$ costs about the same as one full-width head ($4d_{\text{model}}^2$ projection weights either way).
- The three uses of attention (encoder self-attention, masked decoder self-attention, encoder–decoder attention) and where $Q$, $K$, $V$ come from in each.
- The sinusoidal positional encoding, and the finding that learned position embeddings work equally well (Table 3, row E).
- Table 1: self-attention has $O(1)$ sequential operations and $O(1)$ path length, versus $O(n)$ and $O(n)$ for a recurrent layer; per-layer cost $O(n^2 d)$ versus $O(n d^2)$, so self-attention is cheaper when $n < d$.
- The training recipe: Adam ($\beta_2 = 0.98$), a learning rate that warms up for 4,000 steps then decays as $1/\sqrt{\text{step}}$, residual dropout 0.1, label smoothing 0.1.
- Which ablations mattered: one head is 0.9 BLEU worse than eight; smaller $d_k$ hurts; bigger models and dropout help.

## 1 · The problem and the claim

Recurrent models (LSTMs, GRUs) "factor computation along the symbol positions": hidden state $h_t$ is a function of $h_{t-1}$ and input $t$. That is the bottleneck the course met with the [[7-transformers/sequence-to-sequence-rnns|sequence-to-sequence RNN]]: the steps within one sentence cannot run in parallel, and at long sequence lengths memory limits how many sentences can be batched instead. Attention mechanisms already helped such models by connecting positions "without regard to their distance", but they were used on top of a recurrent network.

The paper's proposal is the reverse: an architecture "eschewing recurrence and instead relying entirely on an attention mechanism to draw global dependencies between input and output". The headline is in the introduction: a new state of the art in translation quality "after being trained for as little as twelve hours on eight P100 GPUs".

## 2 · Background

**Convolutional sequence models.** The Extended Neural GPU, ByteNet and ConvS2S also avoid recurrence by computing all positions in parallel with convolutions. But the number of operations needed to relate two positions grows with their distance — linearly for ConvS2S, logarithmically for ByteNet — which makes distant dependencies harder to learn. In the Transformer it is a constant, "albeit at the cost of reduced effective resolution due to averaging attention-weighted positions", which multi-head attention is designed to counteract.

**Attention before Transformers.** The best encoder–decoders already used attention between decoder and encoder: [Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.0473) argued that squeezing a whole source sentence into one fixed-length vector is a bottleneck, and let the decoder "(soft-)search" the encoder's states at every output step — the fix for limitation 3 of the [[7-transformers/sequence-to-sequence-rnns|S2S RNN]]. **Self-attention** (attention among the positions of one sequence) had been used for reading comprehension, summarization and sentence representations. The Transformer is, in the authors' words, "the first transduction model relying entirely on self-attention to compute representations of its input and output without using sequence-aligned RNNs or convolution".

## 3 · Model architecture

![[l19-transformer.png|The Transformer: encoder (left) and decoder (right), the paper's Figure 1 as reproduced in Lecture 19]]

The paper's Figure 1, as reproduced in Lecture 19. The **encoder** maps the input symbols $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ to continuous representations $\mathbf z = (z_1,\dots,z_n)$; the **decoder** generates $(y_1,\dots,y_m)$ one symbol at a time, **auto-regressively**, consuming the symbols it has already produced.

### 3.1 · Encoder and decoder stacks

- **Encoder**: $N = 6$ identical layers, each with two sub-layers — multi-head self-attention, then a position-wise feed-forward network.
- **Decoder**: $N = 6$ identical layers with a third sub-layer in between that attends over the encoder's output. Its self-attention is **masked** so that position $i$ cannot attend to later positions; together with the output embeddings being shifted right by one position, this makes the prediction for position $i$ depend only on outputs before $i$ (the course's [[7-transformers/sequence-to-sequence-rnns|teacher forcing]] set-up).
- **Around every sub-layer**: a residual connection followed by layer normalization,

$$
\text{output} = \mathrm{LayerNorm}\bigl(x + \mathrm{Sublayer}(x)\bigr),
$$

  the "Add & Norm" boxes in the figure ([[7-transformers/layer-norm-and-feed-forward|layer norm and feed-forward]], [[5-deep-networks/residual-connections|residual connections]]). For the sums to work, every sub-layer and the embeddings produce vectors of the same width, $d_{\text{model}} = 512$.

### 3.2.1 · Scaled dot-product attention

An attention function maps a query and a set of key–value pairs to a weighted sum of the values, with weights from a compatibility function of the query and each key. The paper's version, with queries and keys of dimension $d_k$ and values of dimension $d_v$, packed as rows of $Q$, $K$, $V$:

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V. \tag{1}
$$

The [[7-transformers/attention|attention page]] works through this equation and its shapes. Two points come from the paper itself:

- **Why dot products, not additive attention.** Additive attention (Bahdanau's) scores query–key pairs with a one-hidden-layer network. The two have similar theoretical complexity, but dot-product attention "is much faster and more space-efficient in practice" because it is one matrix multiplication.
- **Why divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$.** Without scaling, additive attention beats dot-product attention for large $d_k$. The authors' explanation (footnote 4): if the components of $q$ and $k$ are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, then $q\cdot k = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i$ has mean 0 and variance $d_k$. Large dot products push the softmax into regions with extremely small gradients; dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ brings the variance back to 1.

### 3.2.2 · Multi-head attention

Instead of one attention with $d_{\text{model}}$-wide queries, keys and values, the model projects them $h$ times with different learned matrices and runs $h$ attentions in parallel:

$$
\mathrm{MultiHead}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_1,\dots,\mathrm{head}_h)\,W^O,\qquad \mathrm{head}_i = \mathrm{Attention}(QW_i^Q,\ KW_i^K,\ VW_i^V),
$$

with $W_i^Q, W_i^K\in\mathbb R^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_k}$, $W_i^V\in\mathbb R^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_v}$ and $W^O\in\mathbb R^{hd_v\times d_{\text{model}}}$. The paper uses $h = 8$ and $d_k = d_v = d_{\text{model}}/h = 64$. The reason given: "Multi-head attention allows the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces at different positions. With a single attention head, averaging inhibits this."

> [!key] Same cost as one wide head
> With $d_k = d_v = d_{\text{model}}/h$, the projection weights are $h\,(2\,d_{\text{model}}d_k + d_{\text{model}}d_v) + h\,d_v\,d_{\text{model}} = 4\,d_{\text{model}}^2$ for **any** $h$ — for the base model $8\,(3\cdot512\cdot64) + 512\cdot512 = 1{,}048{,}576 = 4\cdot512^2$, the same as a single head with $d_k = d_v = 512$. The paper: "the total computational cost is similar to that of single-head attention with full dimensionality". Details on [[7-transformers/multi-head-attention|multi-head attention]].

### 3.2.3 · Three uses of attention

| Where | Queries from | Keys and values from | Mask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Encoder self-attention | previous encoder layer | previous encoder layer | none: every position sees every position |
| Decoder self-attention | previous decoder layer | previous decoder layer | causal: position $i$ sees positions $\le i$ |
| Encoder–decoder ("cross") attention | previous decoder layer | **output of the encoder** | none |

The causal mask is implemented "inside of scaled dot-product attention by masking out (setting to $-\infty$) all values in the input of the softmax which correspond to illegal connections", to "prevent leftward information flow in the decoder". The course builds exactly this mask with `torch.triu` ([[7-transformers/attention|masked self-attention]]).

### 3.3 · Position-wise feed-forward networks

Each layer also has a two-layer network applied to every position separately and identically:

$$
\mathrm{FFN}(x) = \max(0,\ xW_1 + b_1)\,W_2 + b_2, \tag{2}
$$

with input and output width $d_{\text{model}} = 512$ and inner width $d_{ff} = 2048$. The weights are shared across positions but differ from layer to layer; the paper notes this is the same as two convolutions with kernel size 1 ([[7-transformers/layer-norm-and-feed-forward|layer norm and feed-forward]]).

### 3.4 · Embeddings and softmax

Input and output tokens become $d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional vectors through learned [[7-transformers/text-encodings-and-embeddings|embeddings]], and a linear layer plus softmax turns the decoder's output into next-token probabilities. Two details: the **same weight matrix** is shared by the two embedding layers and the pre-softmax linear layer (possible because the English–German experiments use one shared source–target vocabulary, §5.1), and in the embedding layers the weights are **multiplied by $\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}$**.

### 3.5 · Positional encoding

Attention by itself ignores order (the [[7-transformers/positional-encoding|positional encoding]] page shows why), so a $d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional position vector is added to each embedding at the bottom of both stacks:

$$
PE_{(pos,\,2i)} = \sin\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right),\qquad PE_{(pos,\,2i+1)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right).
$$

Each pair of dimensions $(2i, 2i+1)$ is a sinusoid of one frequency, and the wavelengths form a geometric progression from $2\pi$ to $10000\cdot2\pi$. The authors chose it because "for any fixed offset $k$, $PE_{pos+k}$ can be represented as a linear function of $PE_{pos}$", which they hypothesized would make attending by relative position easy, and because it "may allow the model to extrapolate to sequence lengths longer than the ones encountered during training". Learned position embeddings gave "nearly identical results" (Table 3, row E).

> [!derivation]- Why a shift is a linear map
> Write $\omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d_{\text{model}}}$. By the angle-addition formulas,
> $$
> \begin{bmatrix}\sin\omega_i(pos+k)\\ \cos\omega_i(pos+k)\end{bmatrix}
> = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\omega_i k & \sin\omega_i k\\ -\sin\omega_i k & \cos\omega_i k\end{bmatrix}
> \begin{bmatrix}\sin\omega_i\,pos\\ \cos\omega_i\,pos\end{bmatrix}.
> $$
> The matrix depends on the offset $k$ but not on $pos$, so stacking one such $2\times2$ rotation per frequency gives a fixed linear map from $PE_{pos}$ to $PE_{pos+k}$. This works because the sine and cosine of a pair share the frequency $\omega_i$.

## 4 · Why self-attention

The paper compares a self-attention layer with recurrent and convolutional layers mapping a length-$n$ sequence of $d$-dimensional vectors to another, on three criteria: total computation per layer, how much of it can run in parallel (the minimum number of **sequential** operations), and the **maximum path length** a signal travels between any two positions — shorter paths make long-range dependencies easier to learn.

| Layer type | Complexity per layer | Sequential operations | Maximum path length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self-attention | $O(n^2\cdot d)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| Recurrent | $O(n\cdot d^2)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
| Convolutional (kernel $k$) | $O(k\cdot n\cdot d^2)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(\log_k n)$ |
| Self-attention, restricted to $r$ neighbours | $O(r\cdot n\cdot d)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(n/r)$ |

- **Complexity.** Self-attention compares all $n^2$ pairs of $d$-vectors; a recurrent layer multiplies a $d\times d$ matrix into each of $n$ states. Self-attention is cheaper when $n < d$, "which is most often the case with sentence representations" built from word pieces or byte pairs.
- **Sequential operations.** A recurrent layer must take $n$ steps in order; self-attention computes every position at once.
- **Path length.** In self-attention every position reaches every other in one step. A single convolution with $k < n$ does not connect all pairs: it takes $O(n/k)$ stacked layers (or $O(\log_k n)$ with dilated convolutions).
- **Restricted self-attention** (only a neighbourhood of size $r$) would scale to very long sequences at the price of an $O(n/r)$ path; the paper leaves it to future work.

## 5 · Training

### 5.1 · Data and batching

| | English→German | English→French |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data | WMT 2014, about 4.5 million sentence pairs | WMT 2014, 36 million sentences |
| Tokens | byte-pair encoding, shared source–target vocabulary of about 37,000 | 32,000 word-piece vocabulary |

Sentence pairs were batched by approximate length, each batch holding about 25,000 source tokens and 25,000 target tokens. Byte-pair and word-piece vocabularies are the subword tokenizers mentioned on the [[7-transformers/tokenization|tokenization]] page: frequent words stay whole and rare words split into pieces, so there are no out-of-vocabulary words.

### 5.2 · Hardware and schedule

One machine with 8 NVIDIA P100 GPUs. Base model: 0.4 s per step, 100,000 steps, 12 hours. Big model: 1.0 s per step, 300,000 steps, 3.5 days.

### 5.3 · Optimizer: Adam with warmup

[Adam](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.Adam.html) with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.98$, $\epsilon = 10^{-9}$ (PyTorch's defaults are $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$), and a learning rate that changes every step:

$$
lrate = d_{\text{model}}^{-0.5}\cdot\min\bigl(step^{-0.5},\ step\cdot warmup\_steps^{-1.5}\bigr),\qquad warmup\_steps = 4000. \tag{3}
$$

For $step < 4000$ the second term is smaller, so the rate grows **linearly**; afterwards the first term is smaller and the rate decays like $1/\sqrt{step}$. The two meet at $step = warmup\_steps$, where for the base model

$$
lrate_{\max} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{512}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{4000}} = \frac{1}{22.63\times63.25} \approx 6.99\times10^{-4}.
$$

The rate grows linearly for 4,000 steps, peaks at <i>d</i><sub>model</sub><sup>−1/2</sup> · 4000<sup>−1/2</sup> ≈ 6.99 × 10⁻⁴ for the base model, then decays like 1/√step (1.40 × 10⁻⁴ at the base model's last step, 100k). The big model's larger <i>d</i><sub>model</sub> = 1024 scales the whole curve down by 1/√2.</figcaption></figure>

In PyTorch this is a [`LambdaLR`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR.html) scheduler stepped once per batch (see [[4-pytorch-training/learning-rate-schedulers|learning-rate schedulers]] and [[4-pytorch-training/optimizers|optimizers]]):

```python
d_model, warmup = 512, 4000
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1.0, betas=(0.9, 0.98), eps=1e-9)
rate = lambda step: d_model**-0.5 * min((step+1)**-0.5, (step+1) * warmup**-1.5)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(opt, lr_lambda=rate)  # lr = 1.0 * rate
# each batch: loss.backward(); opt.step(); sched.step()
```

(`LambdaLR` multiplies the base `lr` by the lambda, so the base is set to 1; `step+1` avoids $0^{-0.5}$ at the first call. This is the approach of [The Annotated Transformer](https://nlp.seas.harvard.edu/annotated-transformer/).)

### 5.4 · Regularization

- **Residual dropout**, $P_{drop} = 0.1$ for the base model: [[5-deep-networks/dropout|dropout]] on the output of every sub-layer before it is added to the sub-layer's input and normalized, and on the sums of embeddings and positional encodings.
- **Label smoothing**, $\epsilon_{ls} = 0.1$ ([Szegedy et al., 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.00567)): the cross-entropy target is no longer one-hot. With vocabulary size $V$, the true token gets probability $1-\epsilon_{ls} + \epsilon_{ls}/V$ and every other token $\epsilon_{ls}/V$. The model is trained to be less certain; the paper reports this "hurts perplexity, as the model learns to be more unsure, but improves accuracy and BLEU score". In PyTorch: [`nn.CrossEntropyLoss(label_smoothing=0.1)`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss.html) ([[3-linear-models/multiclass-logistic-regression|cross-entropy]]).

- **(A) heads**, at constant computation: a single head is 0.9 BLEU worse than the best setting, and quality also drops with too many heads.

### 6.3 · English constituency parsing

To test generality, a 4-layer Transformer with $d_{\text{model}} = 1024$ was trained to output parse trees, whose output is much longer than the input and strongly structured. With only the WSJ training set (about 40,000 sentences) it scored 91.3 F1 on WSJ section 23, and 92.7 with about 17 million extra sentences (semi-supervised). The paper summarizes this as better than all previously reported models except the Recurrent Neural Network Grammar, achieved with little task-specific tuning, and in a small-data setting where RNN sequence-to-sequence models had not reached state of the art.

## 7 · Conclusion

The Transformer is "the first sequence transduction model based entirely on attention", it trains significantly faster than recurrent or convolutional architectures, and it set a new state of the art on both WMT 2014 tasks. The authors planned to apply it to other modalities (images, audio, video), to study local, restricted attention for large inputs, and to make generation less sequential. Their code was released in [tensor2tensor](https://github.com/tensorflow/tensor2tensor).

## Counting the base model's parameters

Table 3 lists about 65 million parameters for the base model. Counting with $d = d_{\text{model}} = 512$, $d_{ff} = 2048$, biases included, and one embedding matrix shared by encoder, decoder and output layer:

| Piece | Formula | Count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| one multi-head attention ($W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O$ + biases) | $4d^2 + 4d$ | 1,050,624 |
| one feed-forward network | $2\,d\,d_{ff} + d_{ff} + d$ | 2,099,712 |
| one LayerNorm ($\gamma$, $\beta$) | $2d$ | 1,024 |
| encoder layer: attention + FFN + 2 LayerNorms | | 3,152,384 |
| decoder layer: 2 attentions + FFN + 3 LayerNorms | | 4,204,032 |
| 6 encoder layers | | 18,914,304 |
| 6 decoder layers | | 25,224,192 |
| shared embedding, $V \approx 37{,}000$ | $V\cdot d$ | 18,944,000 |
| **total** | | **≈ 63.1 million** |

That is close to the reported 65M; the vocabulary size is only "about 37,000", and the paper does not list every bias, so an exact match is not expected (65M would correspond to $V\approx 40{,}700$). The same count for the big model ($d = 1024$, $d_{ff} = 4096$) gives $75{,}577{,}344 + 100{,}780{,}032 + 37{,}888{,}000 \approx 214.2$ million against the reported 213M. Note where the parameters sit: the embedding alone is 30% of the base model, and each decoder layer is a third larger than an encoder layer because of its second attention.

## The paper vs the ECE 364 implementation

Lectures 19–20 build the same architecture at a scale that trains on a laptop, and simplify on purpose. Knowing the differences avoids attributing the course's code to the paper:

| | Paper (base model) | ECE 364 Full Transformer (Lecture 20) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Layers | $N = 6$ encoder + 6 decoder layers | 1 encoder layer + 1 decoder layer (stacking is described, not built) |
| Width | $d_{\text{model}} = 512$ | `embedding_dim = 128`; queries and keys first projected to `hidden_dim = 64` |
| Heads | $h = 8$, $d_k = d_v = 64$ | `n_heads = 4`: queries/keys 16 per head, values 32 per head |
| Q, K, V projections | one $W_i^Q, W_i^K, W_i^V$ per head from $d_{\text{model}}$ | `fc_Q`/`fc_K`/`fc_V`, then a second per-head `nn.Linear` (two linear maps in a row, equivalent to one) |
| Feed-forward | $d_{ff} = 2048 = 4\,d_{\text{model}}$ | `ffn_dim = 2 * embedding_dim = 256` |
| Positional encoding | pair $(2i, 2i+1)$ shares the frequency $10000^{-2i/d}$ | exponent $i/d$ for every index $i$, so odd indices use a slightly different frequency than the paper; `'learned'` and `None` also available |
| Add & Norm | a LayerNorm per sub-layer (as in `nn.TransformerEncoderLayer`) | one `nn.LayerNorm` module reused at every Add & Norm in a block |
| Extra nonlinearity | none after the encoder stack | ReLU applied to the encoder's output |
| Embeddings | one matrix shared by encoder, decoder and output layer, scaled by $\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}$ | separate English and French `nn.Embedding`s and a separate `fc_predict`; no scaling |
| Tokens | subwords: BPE (37k shared) or word-pieces (32k) | whole words with `min_freq = 50`: 1,443 English and 1,646 French words, separate vocabularies |
| Data | 4.5M (EN–DE) / 36M (EN–FR) sentence pairs | `eng-fra.txt`: 37,756 pairs at `min_freq = 50`, split 18,878 / 18,878 |
| Batching | ~25k source + ~25k target tokens per batch, grouped by length | one sentence at a time (losses of a 64-sentence mini-batch averaged in a loop) |
| Optimizer | Adam ($\beta_2 = 0.98$, $\epsilon = 10^{-9}$) with warmup schedule (eq. 3) | SGD, `lr = 1e-2`, `momentum = 0.99`, constant rate in the current notebook; the saved curves the lecture plots ("1 million iterations", "3 million iterations") match last year's loop, plain SGD on one sentence per step (see [[7-transformers/transformer-architecture#results\|results]]) |
| Regularization | residual dropout 0.1, label smoothing 0.1 | none |
| Decoding | beam search, beam 4, length penalty 0.6, max length input + 50 | greedy (`topk(1)`) up to `MAX_LENGTH = 25` or `<EOS>` |
| Metric | BLEU on newstest2014, 0–100 scale | SacreBLEU from `torchmetrics` on 0–1 scale, 1,000 random sentences, all reference translations of the same English sentence |

The course's best validation BLEU (about 0.45–0.54 for the Full Transformer, see [[7-transformers/transformer-architecture|transformer architecture]]) is on short, frequent-word sentences and a different test set, so it cannot be compared with the paper's 27–41.

## Where it leads

Beyond this course: the encoder stack alone became the basis of models such as [BERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805), the decoder stack alone the basis of GPT-style language models, and the [Vision Transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11929) applies the encoder to sequences of image patches — the patch tokens previewed on the [[7-transformers/tokenization#beyond-words|tokenization page]]. Transformers also replaced the U-Net inside image generators: diffusion transformers ([[8-diffusion-models/modern-image-generators|modern image generators]]) cut a noisy image latent into patches, treat them as tokens, and condition on the text prompt with attention.

> [!question]- Check yourself
> 1. For the base model, what are $d_k$ and $d_v$, and how many weights (no biases) do the four projection matrices of one multi-head attention layer hold? How does that compare with a single head of width 512?
> 2. What is the base model's learning rate at step 1,000 and at step 16,000?
> 3. Why must the decoder's self-attention be masked while the encoder's is not?
> 4. Using Table 1, for a 50-token sentence and $d = 512$, which layer type has the smaller per-layer cost, and by roughly what factor?
> 5. With label smoothing $\epsilon_{ls} = 0.1$ and a vocabulary of 37,000, what target probability does the correct token get?
> 6. Which Table 3 row would you cite to argue that the exact positional-encoding formula does not matter much, and which to argue that more than one head helps?
>
> > [!solution]- Answers
> > 1. $d_k = d_v = 512/8 = 64$. Weights: $8\,(3\cdot512\cdot64) + 512\cdot512 = 1{,}048{,}576 = 4\cdot512^2$, exactly the same as one head with $d_k = d_v = 512$.
> > 2. Step 1,000 is in the warmup: $512^{-0.5}\cdot1000\cdot4000^{-1.5}\approx1.75\times10^{-4}$. Step 16,000 is in the decay: $512^{-0.5}\cdot16000^{-0.5}\approx3.49\times10^{-4}$ (half the peak, since 16,000 is four times 4,000).
> > 3. The decoder is trained on the whole shifted target sentence at once; without the mask, position $i$ could read the very token it is supposed to predict (and later ones), which is not available when generating. The encoder sees the complete source sentence both in training and at test time, so nothing needs hiding.
> > 4. Self-attention: $n^2 d$ vs $n d^2$, ratio $n/d = 50/512 \approx 0.1$, so self-attention is about 10 times cheaper (and needs no sequential steps).
> > 5. $0.9 + 0.1/37000 \approx 0.900003$; every other token gets $0.1/37000 \approx 2.7\times10^{-6}$.
> > 6. Row (E): learned embeddings 25.7 BLEU vs 25.8 for sinusoids. Row (A): one head 24.9 BLEU vs 25.8 with eight.
