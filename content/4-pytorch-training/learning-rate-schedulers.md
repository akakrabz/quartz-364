*Lectures 10 and 22 · [[4-pytorch-training/index|PyTorch training]] · builds on [[4-pytorch-training/optimizers|optimizers]] and [[2-calculus-and-optimization/gradient-descent|step size in gradient descent]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> A large learning rate makes fast progress early but bounces around the minimum late; a small one is precise but slow. So in practice the rate is **lowered during training**, most simply by a factor (e.g. 10×) whenever the loss or validation accuracy plateaus. A scheduler from [`torch.optim.lr_scheduler`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/optim.html#how-to-adjust-learning-rate) wraps the optimizer and changes its `lr` each time you call `scheduler.step()` — usually once per epoch, after the epoch's `optimizer.step()` calls.

## What it is

Lecture 10 only introduces the idea ("we will not worry about these for now"); Lecture 22 returns to it with a full experiment ([[#multisteplr-in-lecture-22|below]]). The idea: after the loss plateaus, a smaller learning rate lets gradient descent settle more carefully into the minimum it is already in and squeeze out a little more performance. The [[2-calculus-and-optimization/gradient-descent|gradient-descent]] picture explains why: with step $\alpha$ the iterates near a minimum of curvature $c$ jump by a factor $1-\alpha c$ each step, and with noisy mini-batch gradients they keep jittering at a level that shrinks with $\alpha$.

A scheduler is an object built from the optimizer:

```python
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1, momentum=0.9)
scheduler = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.StepLR(optimizer, step_size=30, gamma=0.1)

for epoch in range(100):
    for x, y in train_loader:
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss = criterion(model(x), y)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
    scheduler.step()                        # once per epoch
    print(epoch, scheduler.get_last_lr())
```

## What you should know

- Lowering the learning rate late in training is standard; the simplest rule is "divide by 10 when the loss plateaus".
- Order: `optimizer.step()` (per batch) **then** `scheduler.step()` (per epoch, for the epoch-based schedulers).
- [`StepLR(optimizer, step_size, gamma)`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.StepLR.html): $\alpha_e = \alpha_0\,\gamma^{\lfloor e/\text{step\_size}\rfloor}$.
- [`ExponentialLR(optimizer, gamma)`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ExponentialLR.html): $\alpha_e = \alpha_0\gamma^e$.
- [`ReduceLROnPlateau(optimizer, factor=0.1, patience=10)`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ReduceLROnPlateau.html) is the lecture's "lower it when it plateaus": call `scheduler.step(val_loss)` with the metric.
- Read the current rate with `scheduler.get_last_lr()` or `optimizer.param_groups[0]['lr']`; you can also set the latter by hand.
- [`MultiStepLR(optimizer, milestones, gamma)`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.MultiStepLR.html), the scheduler of Lecture 22: multiply by $\gamma$ each time the epoch counter reaches a milestone. With `milestones=[70, 85]`, `gamma=0.1` and $\alpha_0 = 10^{-2}$: $10^{-2}$ for epochs 1–70, $10^{-3}$ for 71–85, $10^{-4}$ for 86–100.
- Reducing the rate is the common case; some schedules also raise or restart it ([`CyclicLR`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CyclicLR.html), [`CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts.html)).

## From the lecture

*From the class recording (Lecture 22), paraphrased.*

- Everything so far used a fixed learning rate, found by trying a logarithmic grid such as $10^{-1}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}$. Scheduling changes the rate **during** training.
- Three kinds of trigger: a fixed milestone (a chosen epoch), a metric that stops improving (for example validation performance flat for three epochs), or a function that changes the rate every epoch.
- Every scheduler takes the optimizer as its first argument, then its own settings. `scheduler.step()` is to the scheduler what `optimizer.step()` is to the optimizer: it advances an internal counter, which for `MultiStepLR` counts epochs.
- Why reduce: a good starting rate gets the model into a good region of the loss landscape quickly, but its steps are too big to explore the small bumps inside that region. A smaller rate later lets it settle into a lower spot.
- Why raise or restart: to shake the model out of the sub-valley it is in so that it can find a better one.
- The schedulers in the PyTorch list each link to a paper. They are there because research supports them.
- What he sees most often in papers: start very low, ramp up linearly to a maximum (a warm-up), then decay by steps or along a cosine. That is why PyTorch has [`SequentialLR`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.SequentialLR.html) and [`ChainedScheduler`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ChainedScheduler.html) to combine schedules. Choosing among them is where training becomes "an art and less a science".
- Write milestones as fractions of the run (70% and 85% of the epochs), so they move with the run length.
- A drop does not always pay off: sometimes nothing changes, sometimes the gain is modest. In the lecture's run it was large.

> [!tip] Shortcut — reading a MultiStepLR schedule
> Rate during an epoch $= \alpha_0\,\gamma^{m}$, where $m$ is the number of milestones already reached, that is, milestones $\le$ the number of completed epochs.
>
> - The lecture's run: $\alpha_0 = 10^{-2}$, milestones 70 and 85, $\gamma = 0.1$: $10^{-2}\to10^{-3}\to10^{-4}$, changing after epochs 70 and 85.
> - His spoken example: 200 epochs, milestones 100 and 150, from $10^{-1}$: $10^{-1}\to10^{-2}\to10^{-3}$.
> - The notebook's text example: `milestones=[40, 80], gamma=0.2` from $0.1$: $0.1\to0.02\to0.004$.

> [!intuition] Analogy — potholes in the valley
> Blindfolded in a valley, big steps carry you quickly down to a good area but then hop over every small dip in it. Tiny steps from the start would crawl and could get stuck in the first pothole. So: big steps first to find the good area, small steps later to explore its potholes. Restart schedules do the reverse for a moment, a big step to jump out of one sub-valley into another.

| Scheduler | Rule (epoch $e$, start $\alpha_0$) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| [`StepLR`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.StepLR.html) | multiply by $\gamma$ every `step_size` epochs | "÷10 every 30 epochs" |
| [`MultiStepLR`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.MultiStepLR.html) | multiply by $\gamma$ at each epoch in `milestones` | drops at chosen epochs |
| [`ExponentialLR`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ExponentialLR.html) | multiply by $\gamma$ every epoch | smooth decay |
| [`CosineAnnealingLR`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR.html) | $\eta_{\min} + \tfrac12(\alpha_0-\eta_{\min})\big(1+\cos\frac{\pi e}{T_{\max}}\big)$ | smooth decay to $\eta_{\min}$ at $T_{\max}$ |
| [`ReduceLROnPlateau`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.lr_scheduler.ReduceLROnPlateau.html) | multiply by `factor` once more than `patience` epochs in a row show no improvement of the metric you pass | the lecture's plateau rule |

**Check by hand.** `StepLR(step_size=30, gamma=0.1)` from $\alpha_0 = 0.1$: epochs 0–29 use $0.1$, epochs 30–59 use $0.01$, 60–89 use $0.001$, 90–99 use $10^{-4}$. `ExponentialLR(gamma=0.95)` after 10 epochs: $0.1\cdot0.95^{10} = 0.0599$.

```python
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import ReduceLROnPlateau

scheduler = ReduceLROnPlateau(optimizer, mode='min', factor=0.1, patience=5)
for epoch in range(n_epoch):
    train_one_epoch(model, train_loader, optimizer)
    val_loss = evaluate(model, val_loader)
    scheduler.step(val_loss)                # needs the metric
```

Without a scheduler you can do the same by hand:

```python
for g in optimizer.param_groups:
    g['lr'] = g['lr'] / 10
```

One epoch after the drop the training BLEU has gone from 0.75 to 0.89, and validation BLEU settles about 0.10 higher than before (mean of epochs 61–70: 0.42; epochs 76–85: 0.52). The lecturer's reading: more epochs at $10^{-2}$ might get there eventually, but it would take an extraordinary amount of time, because at that step size the model cannot get deeper into the loss landscape. The plateau was a symptom of a rate that had become too high ([[4-pytorch-training/underfitting-and-overfitting#the-three-cases|"flattens early"]]), and the schedule was the cure. The second drop, to $10^{-4}$, changes almost nothing.

The size of the jump depends on the run. Across the lecture's six logged runs (validation BLEU, mean of epochs 61–70 → mean of epochs 76–85):

| Run | Before the drop | After | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $d_{emb}$ = 32, 4 heads | 0.26 | 0.33 | +0.07 |
| $d_{emb}$ = 64, 4 heads | 0.35 | 0.46 | +0.10 |
| $d_{emb}$ = 128, 1 head | 0.42 | 0.52 | +0.10 |
| $d_{emb}$ = 128, 4 heads | 0.46 | 0.51 | +0.05 |
| $d_{emb}$ = 128, 16 heads | 0.48 | 0.52 | +0.04 |
| $d_{emb}$ = 256, 4 heads | 0.55 | 0.56 | +0.01 |

The run that had already fit the training set before epoch 70 (the widest model, training BLEU 0.98) gains almost nothing; the runs still short of it gain 0.04 to 0.10. (Read from the lecture's TensorBoard logs; the curves are on the [[4-pytorch-training/underfitting-and-overfitting#the-lectures-runs-as-examples|diagnosing-fit]] and [[4-pytorch-training/tensorboard-and-logging#reading-the-lectures-six-runs|TensorBoard]] pages.)

### Raising and combining schedules

Not lecture code, but the recipe the lecturer described as the most common in papers, a linear warm-up followed by a decay, looks like this with PyTorch's combinators:

```python
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import LinearLR, CosineAnnealingLR, SequentialLR

warmup = LinearLR(optimizer, start_factor=0.01, total_iters=5)   # 1% -> 100% over 5 epochs
decay = CosineAnnealingLR(optimizer, T_max=95)                   # then cosine to 0
scheduler = SequentialLR(optimizer, schedulers=[warmup, decay], milestones=[5])
```

The [[7-transformers/attention-is-all-you-need|Transformer paper]] trains with exactly this shape: a linear warm-up and then a decay proportional to $1/\sqrt{\text{step}}$.

> [!trap] Common mistakes
> - **`scheduler.step()` inside the batch loop** for an epoch-based schedule: `StepLR(step_size=30)` then drops after 30 *batches*, not 30 epochs.
> - **`scheduler.step()` before `optimizer.step()`**: PyTorch warns, and the first value of the schedule is skipped.
> - **`ReduceLROnPlateau.step()` without the metric** — it needs the number it watches.
> - A schedule does not rescue a bad starting rate: if $\alpha_0$ diverges in the first epoch, decaying it later is too late.
> - Tuning the schedule on the test set; it is a [[4-pytorch-training/experimental-setup|hyperparameter]], tuned on validation data.
