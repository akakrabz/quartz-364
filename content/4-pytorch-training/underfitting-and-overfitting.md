*Lecture 22 · [[4-pytorch-training/index|PyTorch training]] · builds on [[4-pytorch-training/experimental-setup|experimental setup]] (splits, overfitting) and [[4-pytorch-training/training-loop|the training loop]] · not on Midterm 2, but the idea is on past exams*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Plot two things every epoch: how the model does on the data it trains on, and how it does on held-out validation data. **Underfitting**: both are poor and close together, and the loss flattens early or crawls. **Overfitting**: training is near perfect while validation peaks and then gets **worse**. **Proper fit**: training is strong, validation lags behind it but **flattens instead of falling**. Each diagnosis has its own remedies, and choosing among them is trial and error guided by the curves.

## What it is

[[4-pytorch-training/experimental-setup#overfitting|Lecture 11]] introduced the split into training and validation data and the idea of overfitting. Lecture 22 turns that into a working checklist for the final project. It deliberately stays practical: the lecture names the statistical view (the bias–variance trade-off) and sets it aside, describing instead what you **witness** when you train a model and what to try next.

Three quantities carry the diagnosis:

- the **training loss** per epoch, best viewed on a log scale because it spans orders of magnitude;
- the **training metric** (accuracy, BLEU, IoU, …);
- the **validation metric**, on data the model never trains on.

The distance between the last two is the **generalization gap**.

## What you should know

- Underfitting: poor on training **and** validation, small gap; loss decreasing slowly, or flat early.
- Overfitting: training near perfect and still improving; validation stops improving and **degrades**.
- Proper fit: training strong; validation improves, may lag, then **plateaus**. A gap by itself is not overfitting.
- Underfitting remedies: bigger model (deeper, wider, larger embeddings), train longer, change the learning rate or optimizer, larger batches.
- Overfitting remedies: regularize ([[4-pytorch-training/optimizers#weight-decay|weight decay]], [[5-deep-networks/dropout|dropout]], [[5-deep-networks/data-augmentation|augmentation]]), stop earlier and keep the best checkpoint, smaller batches, more data.
- Batch size cuts both ways: larger batches give cleaner gradients (helps a model that struggles to learn), smaller batches give noisier gradients (can make learning more robust).
- Always know the trivial baseline (majority class, or $1/K$) before calling a number good.
- There is no single correct recipe. Set up a reasonable baseline run, then change **one thing at a time** (an ablation study) and compare the curves.

## From the lecture

*From the class recording, paraphrased.*

- The lecture is new to the course and "won't be on your midterm"; it is there to help with the final project: training models with more confidence, diagnosing choices, and knowing the usual practices.
- Underfitting and overfitting are a balance between the model learning enough and the model learning patterns that do not represent the data. He treats it as something you observe in the curves, not through theory.
- In underfitting you see almost **no generalization gap**: training and validation are about the same because the model is not learning much at all. His example: logistic regression on ImageWoof would sit near guessing level on both.
- "Increase the size of the model" means different things for different models: more layers (depth), more channels per convolutional layer or more units (width), or a larger embedding dimension.
- A loss that flattens out early can mean the learning rate is **too high**: the model is not diverging but jumps around the valley without descending. A loss that falls but very slowly can mean it is **too low**.
- Overfitting is the more common and harder case. Modern models are often over-parameterized (more parameters than training examples) and can fit the training data almost perfectly; the loss may fall from around 1–10 to $10^{-4}$.
- His picture of why validation gets worse: early in training the model learns simple features useful on all data; later, sharpening its predictions, it starts exploiting patterns that exist only in the training set, and relying on them misleads it on new data.
- The simplest remedy is to train for less time: watch validation, and keep a **checkpoint of the best epoch**. He called this a very common practice in public code.
- The best remedy, when possible, is more data. Ten images for each of 100 classes cannot represent the classes; a million can. Augmentation enlarges the data set virtually; real data is worth more.
- A model doing excellently on training data "is not necessarily a bad thing". What matters is that validation flattens rather than falls.
- He gave no silver bullet and said so: training deep models is "just as much an art as a science". Start from something reasonable and vary one setting at a time, as in the ablation study at the end of Homework 6.

> [!intuition] Analogy — the blindfolded walker in the valley
> The loss landscape is a valley you explore blindfolded, feeling for the bottom (the same picture as in [[2-calculus-and-optimization/gradient-descent|gradient descent]]). With steps that are too large you do not fly off into space, but you hop from slope to slope and never get lower: an early plateau. With tiny steps you inch along and can get stuck in the first pothole.

## The three cases

| | Underfitting | Overfitting | Proper fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Training metric | poor | close to perfect | strong, even close to perfect |
| Training loss | falls slowly, or flattens early | keeps falling, possibly by orders of magnitude | falls efficiently, then flattens |
| Validation metric | about the same as training; rises slowly or plateaus quickly | improves at first, then stops and **gets worse** | improves steadily, may lag training, then **plateaus** |
| Gap between training and validation | small | large and growing | present but stable |
| What it means | the model is too simple or not trained enough to capture the data | the model is learning patterns specific to the training set | what the model keeps learning on training data does not hurt held-out data |

The word to hold on to is **degrades**. Validation below training is normal: the model never sees the held-out data, so it should do somewhat worse on it. Overfitting is when validation performance turns around and falls while training performance keeps rising.

### What to try

| If the model underfits | If the model overfits |
| --- | --- |
| **Make the model larger:** more layers, wider layers (more channels or units), larger embedding dimension | **Regularize:** [[4-pytorch-training/optimizers#weight-decay|weight decay]] (L2), [[5-deep-networks/dropout|dropout]], [[5-deep-networks/data-augmentation|augmentation]], auxiliary losses |
| **Train longer** | **Train for less time:** stop when validation plateaus; keep the best checkpoint |
| **Change the learning rate** (lower if the loss flattens early, higher if it crawls) or the optimizer | **Decrease the batch size:** noisier gradients can make learning more robust |
| **Increase the batch size:** smaller batches give noisier gradients | **Get more data,** so the training set matches the true distribution better |

Two remarks from the lecture that do not fit the table:

- **An auxiliary loss** is any extra term added to the main loss to push the model toward generally sensible behaviour. [[3-linear-models/ridge-regression|L2 regularization]] is one: main loss plus a penalty on large weights. Another example he gave: require the same class prediction for an image and its mirror image, whether or not that prediction is correct.
- **Larger models sometimes overfit less.** He mentioned, without listing it as a remedy, that making a model bigger can occasionally help against overfitting (it has room for general patterns besides the specific ones), and that very deep models can show "double descent". Both are outside the course.

## Saving the best model

The lecture recommends, for the final project, adding a couple of lines that save a checkpoint whenever validation improves (or every few epochs). Training on a shared GPU can be cut off, and a saved model can be reloaded to resume or to evaluate. A minimal version (not lecture code):

```python
best_val = 0.0
for epoch in range(n_epochs):
    train_one_epoch(model, train_loader, optimizer)
    val_acc = evaluate(model, val_loader)
    if val_acc > best_val:                       # new best epoch
        best_val = val_acc
        torch.save(model.state_dict(), 'best_model.pt')

model.load_state_dict(torch.load('best_model.pt'))   # restore the best epoch
```

[`torch.save`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.save.html) writes the dictionary of parameter tensors returned by [`model.state_dict()`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.Module.html#torch.nn.Module.state_dict); [`load_state_dict`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.Module.html#torch.nn.Module.load_state_dict) puts them back into a model of the same architecture. Keeping the best-validation checkpoint is early stopping without having to guess the stopping epoch in advance. The epoch count chosen this way is a [[4-pytorch-training/experimental-setup|hyperparameter tuned on validation data]], so the final, honest number still comes from a separate test set.

> [!trap] Common mistakes
> - **Calling any gap overfitting.** Training above validation is expected. Look at the **direction** of the validation curve.
> - **Judging from the training curve alone.** A falling training loss says nothing about held-out data.
> - **Reading "flat" as "done".** A loss that stops falling early may only need a smaller learning rate (see the jump at epoch 71 above).
> - **Regularizing an underfit model.** Dropout or weight decay make fitting harder; an underfit model needs capacity or training time first.
> - **Changing several settings at once.** Then you cannot tell which one mattered.
> - **Forgetting the baseline.** 60% accuracy on a data set that is 60% one class is no learning at all.
> - **Comparing noisy points.** The lecture's BLEU values are means over 1,000 random sentences and wobble by about $\pm0.02$ from epoch to epoch; compare averages over several epochs.

> [!question]- Check yourself
> 1. Training accuracy 99%, validation accuracy 71% and unchanged for the last 40 epochs. Overfitting?
> 2. Training accuracy 31%, validation 30% on a 10-class problem, loss flat since epoch 5. Name two different things to try.
> 3. Validation accuracy peaked at epoch 18 and has fallen since; training accuracy is still rising at epoch 60. Which model do you keep, and what would you change in the next run?
>
> > [!solution]- Answers
> > 1. Not by the lecture's definition: validation has plateaued, not degraded. It is a proper fit with a large generalization gap; more data or regularization might narrow it.
> > 2. Underfitting (both low, no gap; guessing would give 10%). Lower the learning rate (a loss flat this early suggests it is too high), or enlarge the model, or train longer once the loss moves again.
> > 3. Overfitting. Keep the epoch-18 checkpoint. Next run: add dropout, weight decay or augmentation, or get more data, and stop near the validation peak.
