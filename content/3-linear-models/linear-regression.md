*Lecture 7 · [[3-linear-models/index|Linear models]] · builds on [[2-calculus-and-optimization/gradients-and-partial-derivatives|partial derivatives]], [[2-calculus-and-optimization/gradient-descent|gradient descent]] and [[2-calculus-and-optimization/matrix-calculus|matrix calculus]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Linear regression predicts a continuous target with a line, $\hat y = mx + b$, and picks $m$ and $b$ to minimize the **mean squared error** over the data. The objective is a convex bowl, so the single point where both partial derivatives vanish is the global minimum. You can find it three ways: solve the $2\times 2$ system from the partials, run gradient descent, or write the problem as $\frac1N\lVert Xw-y\rVert_2^2$ and solve the **normal equations** $X^\top Xw = X^\top y$. The matrix form is the one that carries over to [[3-linear-models/multiple-linear-regression|many features]], [[3-linear-models/basis-function-regression|feature maps]] and [[3-linear-models/ridge-regression|regularization]].

## What it is

A **regression problem** predicts a continuous value: a house price, a disease-progression score, tomorrow's high temperature. (A **classification problem** predicts one of finitely many classes; that is [[3-linear-models/logistic-regression|logistic regression]].) The data is a set of $N$ pairs
$$
\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N},
$$
where $x_i$ is the input, also called the observation or **covariate**, and $y_i$ is the target or ground truth. In one dimension the model is a line, $\hat y_i = mx_i + b$, with slope $m$ and intercept (bias) $b$.

"Best line" needs a number to minimize. Lecture 7 uses the **mean squared error** (MSE):
$$
\min_{m,\,b}\ J(m,b) = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(mx_i + b - y_i\right)^2 .
$$
Each term is a squared **residual** $r_i = mx_i + b - y_i$, the vertical distance from point $i$ to the line. Squaring makes positive and negative errors count the same and makes $J$ a smooth, convex function of $(m, b)$.

## What you should know

- The objective $J(m,b)=\frac1N\sum_i(mx_i+b-y_i)^2$ and why the errors are squared.
- The two partial derivatives, $\frac{\partial J}{\partial m} = \frac2N\sum_i x_i(mx_i+b-y_i)$ and $\frac{\partial J}{\partial b} = \frac2N\sum_i (mx_i+b-y_i)$, and the $2\times2$ system you get by setting both to zero.
- The matrix form: $X$ is $N\times 2$ with rows $[x_i,\ 1]$, $w = [m,\ b]^\top$, and $J(w) = \frac1N\lVert Xw - y\rVert_2^2$.
- The gradient $\nabla_w J = \frac2N X^\top(Xw-y)$ and the **normal equations** $X^\top X w = X^\top y$, so $w^* = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$.
- The gradient-descent update $w^{(k+1)} = w^{(k)} - \alpha\nabla_w J(w^{(k)})$, and that it only approaches $w^*$.
- A constant in front of the objective ($\frac1N$, $\frac12$, or nothing) scales the gradient but does **not** move the minimizer.
- At the optimum the residuals sum to zero and are orthogonal to the inputs: $X^\top(Xw^*-y)=0$.
- How to fit a line to three or four points by hand (below).

## Solution 1: set the partial derivatives to zero

$J$ is a convex quadratic in $(m,b)$, so its minimum is the stationary point. Differentiate under the sum:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial J}{\partial m} &= \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}2x_i(mx_i+b-y_i) = 0 &&\Longrightarrow&& m\sum_i x_i^2 + b\sum_i x_i = \sum_i x_iy_i,\\
\frac{\partial J}{\partial b} &= \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}2(mx_i+b-y_i) = 0 &&\Longrightarrow&& m\sum_i x_i + bN = \sum_i y_i .
\end{aligned}
$$
Two linear equations in two unknowns:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}\sum_i x_i^2 & \sum_i x_i\\ \sum_i x_i & N\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}m\\ b\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\sum_i x_iy_i\\ \sum_i y_i\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Solving it (Cramer's rule) gives the familiar formulas
$$
m^* = \frac{N\sum_i x_iy_i - \sum_i x_i\sum_i y_i}{N\sum_i x_i^2 - \left(\sum_i x_i\right)^2},\qquad b^* = \bar y - m^*\bar x,
$$
where $\bar x$ and $\bar y$ are the averages. The second one says the best line passes through the centroid $(\bar x, \bar y)$.

```python
# Lecture 7: 2x2 system from partials
sum_x2 = np.sum(x**2); sum_x = np.sum(x)
sum_xy = np.sum(x*y);  sum_y = np.sum(y)
C = np.array([[sum_x2, sum_x],
              [sum_x,  N]])
m, b = np.linalg.inv(C) @ np.array([sum_xy, sum_y])
```

This works, but every new parameter means another partial derivative to derive by hand. The matrix form below does all of them at once.

## Solution 2: gradient descent

Put the two partials into a gradient and step downhill, as in [[2-calculus-and-optimization/gradient-descent|gradient descent]]:
$$
\nabla J(m,b) = \begin{bmatrix}\frac2N\sum_i x_i(mx_i+b-y_i)\\[2pt] \frac2N\sum_i (mx_i+b-y_i)\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\begin{aligned} m^{(k+1)} &= m^{(k)} - \alpha\,\tfrac{\partial J}{\partial m},\\ b^{(k+1)} &= b^{(k)} - \alpha\,\tfrac{\partial J}{\partial b}.\end{aligned}
$$
Both partials are evaluated at the **current** $(m^{(k)}, b^{(k)})$ before either parameter is updated.

```python
# Lecture 7: GD on the line, vectorized
m, b = -2.0, -4.0     # initial guess
alpha = 3e-2          # step size
for k in range(200):
    r = m*x + b - y                       # residuals
    dm, db = np.mean(2*x*r), np.mean(2*r)
    m, b = m - alpha*dm, b - alpha*db     # simultaneous update
```

## Solution 3: the matrix form and the normal equations

Stack the data into a matrix with a column of ones for the intercept:
$$
X = \begin{bmatrix}x_1 & 1\\ x_2 & 1\\ \vdots & \vdots\\ x_N & 1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 2},\qquad
w = \begin{bmatrix}m\\ b\end{bmatrix},\qquad
y = \begin{bmatrix}y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_N\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Row $i$ of $Xw - y$ is $mx_i + b - y_i$, so
$$
J(w) = \frac1N\lVert Xw - y\rVert_2^2 = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N (mx_i+b-y_i)^2,
$$
the same objective. Its gradient (the Lecture 3 identity $\nabla_w\lVert Xw-y\rVert_2^2 = 2X^\top(Xw-y)$, see [[2-calculus-and-optimization/matrix-calculus|matrix calculus]]) set to zero gives the normal equations:
$$
\nabla_w J = \frac2N X^\top(Xw - y) = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X^\top X\,w = X^\top y
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad w^* = \left(X^\top X\right)^{-1}X^\top y .
$$

> [!derivation]- Derivation of the gradient by expanding the norm
> $$
> \lVert Xw-y\rVert_2^2 = (Xw-y)^\top(Xw-y) = w^\top X^\top X w - 2y^\top X w + y^\top y .
> $$
> With $\nabla_w(w^\top Aw) = (A+A^\top)w = 2Aw$ for the symmetric $A = X^\top X$, and $\nabla_w(c^\top w) = c$ for $c = X^\top y$:
> $$
> \nabla_w \lVert Xw-y\rVert_2^2 = 2X^\top X w - 2X^\top y = 2X^\top(Xw - y).
> $$
> The $\frac1N$ only scales it. The Hessian $\frac2N X^\top X$ is positive semidefinite, which is why the stationary point is a minimum.

Reading $X^\top(Xw^*-y) = 0$ row by row gives two useful checks: the column of ones says $\sum_i r_i = 0$ (residuals sum to zero), and the $x$ column says $\sum_i x_ir_i = 0$. These are exactly equations (1) and (2) of Solution 1.

```python
# Lecture 7: normal equations in NumPy
X = np.stack((x, np.ones(N)), axis=1)   # (N, 2)
w = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y     # [m, b]
```

On the notebook's data (25 points around $y = 4x + 2$ with noise $\sigma=10$) all three methods agree: the partial-derivative system and the normal equations both print `m = 2.476 | b = 8.466` with `MSE = 62.938`, and gradient descent approaches the same values.

The same formula, unchanged, handles $d$ features: give $X$ one column per feature plus the column of ones. That is [[3-linear-models/multiple-linear-regression|multiple linear regression]].

## Worked example by hand

> [!example] Fit $y = mx + b$ to $(0, 1)$, $(1, 2)$, $(2, 4)$
> $$
> X = \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&1\\2&1\end{bmatrix},\quad
> X^\top X = \begin{bmatrix}5&3\\3&3\end{bmatrix},\quad
> X^\top y = \begin{bmatrix}0+2+8\\ 1+2+4\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}10\\7\end{bmatrix}.
> $$
> $\det(X^\top X) = 15 - 9 = 6$, so
> $$
> w^* = \frac16\begin{bmatrix}3&-3\\-3&5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}10\\7\end{bmatrix} = \frac16\begin{bmatrix}9\\5\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3/2\\ 5/6\end{bmatrix}.
> $$
> The formula version agrees: $m^* = \frac{3\cdot 10 - 3\cdot 7}{3\cdot 5 - 3^2} = \frac96$, and $b^* = \bar y - m^*\bar x = \frac73 - \frac32 = \frac56$.
>
> **Check.** The predictions are $\frac56, \frac73, \frac{23}6$, so the residuals are $-\frac16, \frac13, -\frac16$. They sum to $0$, and $0\cdot(-\frac16) + 1\cdot\frac13 + 2\cdot(-\frac16) = 0$. The MSE is $\frac13\left(\frac1{36}+\frac4{36}+\frac1{36}\right) = \frac1{18}\approx 0.056$.

> [!example] Two steps of gradient descent on the same data
> Start at $w^{(0)} = (0, 0)$ with $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\nabla J = \frac23X^\top(Xw-y)$.
> - Step 1: $Xw^{(0)} - y = (-1,-2,-4)$, so $\nabla J = \frac23(-10, -7) = (-6.667, -4.667)$ and $w^{(1)} = (0.667, 0.467)$.
> - Step 2: $Xw^{(1)} - y = (-0.533, -0.867, -2.2)$, so $\nabla J = \frac23(-5.267, -3.6) = (-3.511, -2.4)$ and $w^{(2)} = (1.018, 0.707)$.
>
> The MSE falls from $7$ to $1.959$ to $0.581$, on its way to $\frac1{18}$ at $(1.5, 0.833)$.

## In PyTorch

The same three ideas in PyTorch: solve the normal equations directly, or let [[2-calculus-and-optimization/autograd|autograd]] compute the gradient.

```python
import torch
x = torch.tensor([0., 1., 2.])
y = torch.tensor([1., 2., 4.])
X = torch.stack((x, torch.ones_like(x)), dim=1)   # (3, 2)

# closed form: solve, don't invert
w = torch.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)          # tensor([1.5000, 0.8333])

# gradient descent with autograd
w = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)
for k in range(500):
    loss = torch.mean((X @ w - y)**2)   # MSE
    loss.backward()
    with torch.no_grad():
        w -= 0.1 * w.grad
    w.grad = None                       # clear accumulated gradient
```

[`torch.linalg.solve`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.linalg.solve.html) solves $X^\top Xw = X^\top y$ without forming an inverse, which is faster and more accurate than [`torch.linalg.inv`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.linalg.inv.html) followed by a product; [`torch.linalg.lstsq`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.linalg.lstsq.html) solves the least-squares problem from $X$ and $y$ directly. In a full model the loss line becomes [`nn.MSELoss`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.MSELoss.html) and the update becomes an optimizer step; see [[4-pytorch-training/loss-functions|loss functions]] and [[4-pytorch-training/optimizers|optimizers]].

> [!trap] Common mistakes
> - **Forgetting the column of ones.** Without it the line is forced through the origin ($b = 0$).
> - **Updating $m$ before computing $\partial J/\partial b$.** Gradient descent uses the gradient at the current point for every coordinate at once.
> - **Thinking the scaling matters.** $\frac1N\lVert Xw-y\rVert^2$, $\frac12\lVert Xw-y\rVert^2$ and $\lVert Xw-y\rVert^2$ have the same minimizer; only the gradient's size (and so a good $\alpha$) changes.
> - **Assuming $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ always exists.** It fails when columns of $X$ are dependent, for instance when there are fewer data points than unknowns. See [[3-linear-models/ridge-regression|ridge regression]].
> - **"Linear" means linear in $w$**, not in $x$: $y\approx w_1x^2 + w_2$ is still linear regression. See [[3-linear-models/basis-function-regression|basis-function regression]].
> - **Using it to classify.** Least squares on $\pm1$ labels breaks on imbalanced data; see [[3-linear-models/logistic-regression|logistic regression]].
