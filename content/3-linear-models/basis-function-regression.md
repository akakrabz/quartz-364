> [!abstract] In one breath
> Replace each input $x_i$ by a feature vector $\phi(x_i)$, for example $[x_i^3, x_i^2, x_i, 1]$, and stack those as the rows of a matrix $\Phi$. The model $\hat y = \phi(x)^\top w$ can curve in $x$, but it is still **linear in the weights** $w$, so it is ordinary least squares with $\Phi$ in place of $X$: $w^* = (\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top y$. Lecture 8 calls this **kernelized linear regression** and $\phi$ the kernel function. The exam question is always: is this model linear in its unknowns, and if so, what is $\Phi$?

## What it is

Let $\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^d$ map a scalar input to $d$ features. Then
$$
\min_w\ J(w) = \frac1N\lVert\Phi w - y\rVert_2^2,\qquad
\Phi = \begin{bmatrix}\phi(x_1)^\top\\ \phi(x_2)^\top\\ \vdots\\ \phi(x_N)^\top\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}.
$$
This is [[3-linear-models/multiple-linear-regression|multiple linear regression]] where the "features" happen to be computed from $x$, so the gradient and solution carry over unchanged:
$$
\nabla_wJ(w) = \frac2N\Phi^\top(\Phi w - y),\qquad w^{(k+1)} = w^{(k)} - \alpha\nabla_wJ,\qquad w^* = \left(\Phi^\top\Phi\right)^{-1}\Phi^\top y .
$$
(The name "kernelized" is the lecture's; in other courses this is usually called basis-function or feature regression, and "kernel" means something more specific.)

## What you should know

- How to write $\Phi$: one row per data point, one column per unknown weight, each entry the function that multiplies that weight.
- The **linear-in-the-weights test**: the model must be a sum of (known function of $x$) $\times$ (one unknown weight). Nonlinear functions of $x$ are fine; unknowns inside $e^{wx}$, $\sin(wx)$ or $x^{w}$ are not.
- A constant term is a column of ones; leave it out only when the model has no intercept.
- A unique solution needs at least as many data points as weights ($N\ge d$) and independent columns of $\Phi$.
- Higher-degree polynomials fit the training data better and better but can swing wildly between points; [[3-linear-models/ridge-regression|ridge regression]] tames them.

## Polynomial regression

A fifth-order polynomial $y_i\approx m_1x_i^5 + m_2x_i^4 + m_3x_i^3 + m_4x_i^2 + m_5x_i + m_6$ has six unknowns, so
$$
\phi(x_i) = \begin{bmatrix}x_i^5\\ x_i^4\\ x_i^3\\ x_i^2\\ x_i\\ 1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\Phi = \begin{bmatrix}
x_1^5 & x_1^4 & x_1^3 & x_1^2 & x_1 & 1\\
x_2^5 & x_2^4 & x_2^3 & x_2^2 & x_2 & 1\\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\
x_N^5 & x_N^4 & x_N^3 & x_N^2 & x_N & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

```python
# Lecture 8: fifth-order polynomial regression
Phi = np.array([[e**5, e**4, e**3, e**2, e, 1] for e in x])
print(Phi.shape)                              # (100, 6)
w = np.linalg.inv(Phi.T @ Phi) @ Phi.T @ y    # six coefficients
```

> [!example] Lecture 12 practice: a cubic
> For $y_i\approx w_3x_i^3 + w_2x_i^2 + w_1x_i + w_0$,
> $$
> \Phi = \begin{bmatrix}
> x_1^3 & x_1^2 & x_1 & 1\\
> x_2^3 & x_2^2 & x_2 & 1\\
> \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\
> x_N^3 & x_N^2 & x_N & 1
> \end{bmatrix}.
> $$
> **Minimum $N$ for a unique solution:** 4, one per unknown. With 3 points, $\Phi$ is $3\times 4$, $\Phi^\top\Phi$ is $4\times 4$ with rank at most 3, and it is singular.

## The linear-in-the-weights test

| model | linear regression? | row $i$ of $\Phi$ |
|---|---|---|
| $y\approx w_1x^2 + w_2x + w_3\sqrt{x}$ (Fall 2025 5b) | yes | $[x_i^2,\ x_i,\ \sqrt{x_i}]$ |
| $y\approx e^{-w_1x} + e^{-w_2x} + e^{-w_3x}$ (Fall 2025 5a) | no: each weight is inside an exponential | — |
| $y\approx A_1\sin\omega_0x + A_2\sin2\omega_0x + A_3\sin3\omega_0x$, $\omega_0$ known (HW3 Q1) | yes, the unknowns are $A_1, A_2, A_3$ | $[\sin\omega_0x_i,\ \sin2\omega_0x_i,\ \sin3\omega_0x_i]$ |
| $y\approx w_3x^3 + w_2x^2 + w_1x + w_0$ (Lecture 12) | yes; needs $N\ge 4$ | $[x_i^3,\ x_i^2,\ x_i,\ 1]$ |
| $y\approx w_1e^{x} + w_2$ | yes | $[e^{x_i},\ 1]$ |
| $y\approx w_1\cos x + w_2x^3 + w_3$ | yes | $[\cos x_i,\ x_i^3,\ 1]$ |
| $y\approx w_1\sin(w_2x)$ | no: $w_2$ is inside the sine | — |
| $y\approx 1 + w_2x^{w_1}$ | no: $w_1$ is an exponent | — |
| $y\approx (w_1 + w_2)x$ | linear, but the two columns of $\Phi$ are both $x_i$, so $\Phi^\top\Phi$ is singular; only $w_1 + w_2$ can be found | $[x_i,\ x_i]$ |

> [!example] Fall 2025 Q5(b): write $\Phi$ for $N = 3$
> $$
> \Phi = \begin{bmatrix}
> x_1^2 & x_1 & \sqrt{x_1}\\
> x_2^2 & x_2 & \sqrt{x_2}\\
> x_3^2 & x_3 & \sqrt{x_3}
> \end{bmatrix},\qquad w = \begin{bmatrix}w_1\\ w_2\\ w_3\end{bmatrix}.
> $$
> There is no column of ones because the model has no constant term. With three unknowns and $N = 3$ distinct positive inputs, $\Phi$ is square and invertible and the fit passes through all three points.

> [!intuition] Why it is still "linear"
> Fix the data. Then $\Phi$ is just a matrix of numbers, and the predictions $\Phi w$ are a linear function of $w$. The objective $\frac1N\lVert\Phi w-y\rVert^2$ is the same convex quadratic bowl as before, with one minimum you can solve for exactly. If a weight sits inside a nonlinearity, the predictions are no longer linear in $w$, the objective is no longer a quadratic, and there is no normal-equation formula; you would need gradient descent and could hit local minima.

## In PyTorch

```python
# cubic features from a (N,) tensor x
Phi = torch.stack((x**3, x**2, x, torch.ones_like(x)), dim=1)  # (N, 4)
Phi = torch.vander(x, N=4)                       # same columns, highest power first
w = torch.linalg.solve(Phi.T @ Phi, Phi.T @ y)  # (4,)
y_hat = Phi @ w
```

[`torch.vander`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.vander.html) builds the polynomial $\Phi$ (powers from $N-1$ down to $0$ by default, matching the lecture's column order). For other features, compute each column and [`torch.stack`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.stack.html) them along `dim=1`.

> [!trap] Common mistakes
> - **Calling a model nonlinear because the curve is.** $w_1x^2 + w_2$ is linear regression. Look at where the *unknowns* sit, not $x$.
> - **Adding a ones column the model does not have** (or forgetting the one it does). Each column of $\Phi$ matches one term of the model.
> - **Too few points.** $N$ must be at least the number of weights, and repeated or dependent columns make $\Phi^\top\Phi$ singular even with many points.
> - **High degree without regularization.** A degree-10 fit to 20 noisy points will overfit; see the figure on [[3-linear-models/ridge-regression|ridge regression]].

> Fall 2025 Q5(a)–(b) (10 points) were exactly the linear-in-the-weights test plus writing $\Phi$ for $N = 3$; Q5(c) showed a fitted polynomial and asked whether to change $\lambda$. HW3 Q1 is the sinusoid version. See [[6-midterm-1/fall-2025|Fall 2025]] and the [[6-midterm-1/practice-problems|practice problems]].
