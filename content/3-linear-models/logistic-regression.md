> [!abstract] In one breath
> Logistic regression is a **classifier**, despite the name. It scores an input linearly, $z = w^\top x$ (with a 1 appended to $x$ for the bias), and turns the score into a probability with the sigmoid: $f_w(x) = \sigma(w^\top x) = \Pr\{y=+1\mid x\}$. It predicts $+1$ exactly when $w^\top x \ge 0$, so the decision boundary $w^\top x = 0$ is a line (a hyperplane). Training maximizes the likelihood of the labels, which is the same as minimizing the **negative log-likelihood** $\sum_i\ln(1+e^{-y_iw^\top x_i})$. There is no closed form; use gradient descent.

## What it is

A **classification problem** assigns each input to one of a fixed set of classes: cat or dog, which digit, will this patient develop diabetes. With two classes it is **binary classification**, with a positive class ($y = +1$) and a negative class ($y = -1$, or $y = 0$ in the [[3-linear-models/binary-cross-entropy|cross-entropy]] convention).

| task | type |
|---|---|
| predict tomorrow's highest temperature (Fall 2025 T/F e) | regression |
| predict the exact price of a house (Spring 2026 T/F b) | regression |
| will a patient develop type-II diabetes next year? (HW3 Q3a) | binary classification |
| a speaker's age from an audio clip (HW3 Q3b) | regression |
| a vehicle's speed from a video clip (HW3 Q3c) | regression |
| which of ten digits is in an image | multi-class classification, see [[3-linear-models/multiclass-logistic-regression\|multi-class logistic regression]] |

## What you should know

- The model $f_w(x) = \sigma(w^\top x) = \dfrac1{1+e^{-w^\top x}} = \Pr\{y = +1\mid x\}$, and $1 - f_w(x) = \sigma(-w^\top x) = \Pr\{y=-1\mid x\}$.
- With $\pm1$ labels both cases are one formula: $\Pr\{y = y_i\mid x_i\} = \sigma(y_iw^\top x_i)$.
- **Decision rule:** predict $+1$ iff $\sigma(w^\top x)\ge 0.5$ iff $w^\top x\ge 0$. Classifying is a sign check; you never need to evaluate $\sigma$.
- The decision boundary $w^\top x = 0$ is linear in $x$; $w$ points toward the positive side. In 1-D with $z = mx+b$ it is the point $x = -b/m$.
- The **margin** $m_i = y_iw^\top x_i$ is positive iff example $i$ is classified correctly; its likelihood is $\sigma(m_i)$, increasing in $m_i$.
- The objective $J(w) = \sum_i\ln\left(1+e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}\right)$, how it comes from maximum likelihood, and its gradient $\nabla_wJ = -\sum_i\dfrac{y_ix_i\,e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}}{1+e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}}$.
- No closed-form solution: train by gradient descent.
- Why least squares on $\pm1$ labels is a bad classifier (imbalanced or skewed data, more than two classes).

## Why not linear regression?

Lecture 8 tried the obvious thing: fit least squares to the labels $\pm1$ and classify with the sign, $\hat y = \mathrm{sgn}(mx + b)$. It works for balanced data symmetric about the gap. It fails in two ways:

1. **Imbalanced or skewed data.** Extra positives far from the boundary pull the least-squares line toward them, and its zero crossing moves into the positive class, misclassifying points of data that is *still linearly separable*. Least squares is trying to hit the value $+1$ exactly, so a correct but large score such as $+20$ costs $(20-1)^2 = 361$, far more than a slightly wrong score near 0 (about 1); the fit gives up correct points near the gap to shrink those big errors.
2. **More than two classes.** Labels $0, 1, 2$ for cat, dog, frog impose an order (and distances) that mean nothing.

Logistic regression fixes the first by predicting a *probability* that saturates at 0 and 1, so far-away points that are already confidently correct barely affect the fit. [[3-linear-models/multiclass-logistic-regression|Multi-class logistic regression]] fixes the second with one score per class.

## The model

Score the input with a linear function, $z_i = mx_i + b$ (in general $z_i = w^\top x_i$ with a 1 appended to $x_i$), and squash the score into $(0, 1)$ with the **sigmoid** (logistic function):
$$
f_w(x_i) = \sigma(z_i) = \frac1{1+e^{-z_i}} = \Pr\{y_i = +1\mid x = x_i\}.
$$
As $z\to+\infty$, $\sigma(z)\to 1$; as $z\to-\infty$, $\sigma(z)\to 0$; and $\sigma(0) = \frac12$.

The probability of the negative class is the complement:
$$
1 - f_w(x_i) = 1 - \frac1{1+e^{-z_i}} = \frac{e^{-z_i}}{1+e^{-z_i}}\cdot\frac{e^{z_i}}{e^{z_i}} = \frac1{1+e^{z_i}} = \sigma(-z_i).
$$
So for a label $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$,
$$
\Pr\{y = y_i\mid x_i\} = \frac1{1+e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}} = \sigma(y_iw^\top x_i).
$$

> [!key] Sigmoid facts worth knowing
> $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$ · $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\left(1-\sigma(z)\right)$ · $\sigma(0) = 0.5$ · $\sigma(2)\approx 0.881$, $\sigma(-2)\approx 0.119$ · $\sigma(z)\ge 0.5 \iff z\ge 0$. More on the sigmoid as a neural-network activation in [[5-deep-networks/activation-functions|activation functions]].

### Decision rule and boundary

Predict the more likely class: $+1$ iff $\sigma(w^\top x)\ge 0.5$ iff $w^\top x\ge 0$. The set $w^\top x = 0$ is the **decision boundary**: a point in 1-D ($x = -b/m$), a line in 2-D, a hyperplane in general. Contours of equal probability are parallel to it.

## Training: maximum likelihood

Choose $w$ to make the observed labels as probable as possible. Assuming the examples are independent, the likelihood is a product:
$$
\max_w\ \Pr\{\mathcal{D}\mid w\} = \max_w\ \prod_{i=1}^{N}\Pr\{y = y_i\mid x_i, w\} = \max_w\ \prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac1{1+e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}} .
$$
Products are awkward to differentiate. Take $\ln$ (monotone, so the maximizer does not move) to get a sum, and negate it to get a minimization. The result is the **negative log-likelihood** (NLL):
$$
\min_w\ J(w) = -\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\frac1{1+e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\left(1 + e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}\right).
$$
Its gradient, term by term with the chain rule:
$$
\nabla_wJ(w) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{-y_ix_i\,e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}}{1+e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}} = -\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_ix_i\,\sigma\!\left(-y_iw^\top x_i\right).
$$
Setting this to zero has **no analytical solution**, so logistic regression is trained with gradient descent. Each example pushes $w$ in the direction $y_ix_i$, weighted by $\sigma(-m_i)$, the probability the model currently assigns to the *wrong* label: badly classified points push hard, confidently correct ones hardly at all.

The same objective, written with $0/1$ labels, is the [[3-linear-models/binary-cross-entropy|binary cross-entropy]] loss.

> [!intuition] Margins
> Call $m_i = y_iw^\top x_i$ the margin of example $i$. It is positive iff the example is on the correct side of the boundary, and the example's likelihood is $\sigma(m_i)$, which increases with $m_i$. So "which $w$ classifies the data" means "which $w$ makes every margin positive", and among those, larger margins mean larger likelihood. (If the data is perfectly separable, scaling $w$ up increases every margin, so the NLL keeps decreasing as $\lVert w\rVert$ grows; in practice you stop after a fixed number of steps or add regularization.)

> [!example] HW3 Q4: sign checks
> - **(a)** 1-D, $w = -3$, no bias: positive iff $-3x\ge 0$, i.e. $x\le 0$.
> - **(b)** $w = (1, -2, 2, -1)$: compute $w^\top x$ for each input; $-1\to$ negative, $2\to$ positive, $-9\to$ negative.
> - **(c)** Positive cluster near $(2,-4)$, negative near $(-3,3)$: $w = (1,-1)$ and $w = (2,-3)$ both work (each gives $(2,-4)$ a positive score and $(-3,3)$ a negative one); $w = (1,1)$ sends $(2,-4)$ to the negative side, and $w = (-2,3)$ flips both classes.

> [!example] Predictions with a bias
> $w = (2, -1, 1)$ acting on $(x_1, x_2, 1)$. For $(1,1)$: $w^\top x = 2-1+1 = 2\to +1$, with $\Pr\{y=+1\} = \sigma(2)\approx 0.881$. For $(0,3)$: $0-3+1 = -2\to -1$. For $(-1,-2)$: $-2+2+1 = 1\to+1$.

## In NumPy (Lecture 9)

The lecture's loop sums the gradient one example at a time; vectorized, the same thing is:

```python
def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

X = np.stack((x, np.ones_like(x)), axis=1)   # append the bias input
w = np.zeros(2)
alpha = 3e-1
for n in range(500):
    margins = y * (X @ w)                     # y in {-1, +1}
    grad = -X.T @ (y * sigmoid(-margins))     # NLL gradient
    w = w - alpha * grad
nll = np.sum(np.log(1 + np.exp(-y * (X @ w))))
```

The lecture exercise applies the same code to MNIST digit pairs (say 0 vs 8): each $28\times28$ image is flattened to a length-784 vector, so $X$ is $1000\times 784$ for 500 images per class, and the model reports **classification accuracy**, the fraction of examples whose predicted class matches the label, on both the training images and 200 held-out test images. That version starts from a random $w$ (`np.random.randn(784)`) and appends no 1 for a bias, which the lecture calls unnecessary for such high-dimensional inputs. The exercise asks you to try other digit pairs (similar shapes are harder to separate) and other $N$ (with few training images, training accuracy can be much higher than test accuracy).

## In PyTorch (Lecture 9)

Autograd replaces the hand-derived gradient. With $0/1$ labels the loss is [`nn.BCELoss`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.BCELoss.html), which takes probabilities:

```python
x_aug = torch.cat((x_coords, torch.ones(len(y), 1)), dim=1)  # (N, 3)
w = torch.zeros(3, requires_grad=True)
loss_fn = torch.nn.BCELoss()        # labels y are 0. / 1.
for n in range(500):
    f_x = torch.sigmoid(x_aug @ w)  # probabilities
    loss = loss_fn(f_x, y)
    loss.backward()
    with torch.no_grad():
        w -= 1e-2 * w.grad
    w.grad = None
```

From Lecture 10 on, the same model is an [`nn.Linear(d, 1)`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.Linear.html) inside an [[4-pytorch-training/nn-module|nn.Module]], trained by an [[4-pytorch-training/optimizers|optimizer]] in the standard [[4-pytorch-training/training-loop|training loop]]; [`nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss.html) then takes the raw score and applies the sigmoid itself.

> [!trap] Common mistakes
> - **The name.** Logistic regression is for classification. "Predict tomorrow's temperature with logistic regression" is false.
> - **Evaluating the sigmoid to classify.** Only the sign of $w^\top x$ matters.
> - **Forgetting the bias input.** Without a 1 appended to $x$, the boundary is forced through the origin.
> - **Mixing label conventions.** The NLL $\sum\ln(1+e^{-y_iw^\top x_i})$ needs $y\in\{-1,+1\}$; `nn.BCELoss` needs float targets in $\{0, 1\}$.
> - **Looking for a closed form.** There is none; use gradient descent.
> - **Thinking the boundary is curved.** It is the hyperplane $w^\top x = 0$. A curved boundary needs nonlinear features or a [[5-deep-networks/multilayer-perceptrons|multilayer perceptron]].
> - **Gradient *ascent* vs descent.** Maximizing the likelihood is gradient ascent on $\ln\Pr$, or equivalently gradient descent on the NLL. Keep the sign straight.
