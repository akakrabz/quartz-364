> [!abstract] In one breath
> Ridge regression adds a penalty on the size of the weights: $\min_w \lVert Xw-y\rVert_2^2 + \lambda\lVert w\rVert_2^2$ with $\lambda > 0$. Setting the gradient $2X^\top(Xw-y) + 2\lambda w$ to zero gives $w^* = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$, which **always exists**, even when $d > N$. Larger $\lambda$ means smaller weights and smoother fits (risk: underfitting); smaller $\lambda$ means closer to plain least squares (risk: overfitting). It also reduces the pull of outliers.

## Why regularize

Three problems from Lecture 8, one fix:

1. **The closed form can fail.** If $X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ has $d > N$, its rank is at most $N$, so the $d\times d$ matrix $X^\top X$ has rank at most $N < d$: it is singular and $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ does not exist. The same happens with dependent columns.
2. **Outliers drag the fit.** A few corrupted points can pull least squares far from the bulk of the data.
3. **Flexible models overfit.** Fitting a high-degree polynomial "to be safe" gives huge, alternating coefficients and a curve that swings wildly between data points.

**Regularization** constrains the solution using prior knowledge. L2 regularization encodes "the weights should be moderate in size, with no single weight exploding".

## What you should know

- The objective $\lVert Xw-y\rVert_2^2 + \lambda\lVert w\rVert_2^2$, with $\lambda>0$ a **hyperparameter** you choose (it is not learned from the training loss).
- Its gradient $2\left(X^\top(Xw-y) + \lambda w\right)$ and solution $w^* = (X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$, derived below.
- How the constants change the answer: with $\frac12\lVert\Phi w-y\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ the solution is $(\Phi^\top\Phi + 2\lambda I)^{-1}\Phi^\top y$. Derive it; don't memorize one version.
- Why $X^\top X + \lambda I$ is always invertible.
- $\lambda = 0$ is ordinary least squares; $\lambda\to\infty$ drives $w\to 0$.
- Reading a plot: wild swings mean increase $\lambda$, a flat line that misses the trend means decrease $\lambda$.
- In gradient descent the penalty becomes **weight decay**: each step shrinks $w$ toward 0.

## Objective, gradient, closed form

$$
\min_w\ J(w) = \lVert Xw - y\rVert_2^2 + \lambda\lVert w\rVert_2^2,\qquad \lambda > 0 .
$$
(The lecture drops the $\frac1N$; it only rescales $\lambda$, which you set by hand anyway.) Using $\nabla_w\lVert w\rVert_2^2 = 2w$:
$$
\nabla_wJ(w) = 2X^\top(Xw - y) + 2\lambda w = 2\left(X^\top(Xw-y) + \lambda w\right).
$$
Set it to zero:
$$
X^\top Xw + \lambda w = X^\top y
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (X^\top X + \lambda I)\,w = X^\top y
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad w^* = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y .
$$

> [!key] Why $X^\top X + \lambda I$ is invertible
> For any $v\ne 0$, $v^\top(X^\top X + \lambda I)v = \lVert Xv\rVert_2^2 + \lambda\lVert v\rVert_2^2 \ge \lambda\lVert v\rVert_2^2 > 0$. So $(X^\top X+\lambda I)v\ne 0$ for every $v\ne0$: the matrix has no null space. Equivalently, every eigenvalue of $X^\top X$ is $\ge 0$ and adding $\lambda I$ shifts them all to $\ge\lambda > 0$. This holds for every $N$ and $d$, including $d > N$.

### Different constants, different formula

| objective | gradient set to 0 | solution |
|---|---|---|
| $\lVert Xw-y\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ (Lecture 8) | $2X^\top(Xw-y) + 2\lambda w = 0$ | $(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ |
| $\frac12\lVert Xw-y\rVert^2 + \frac\lambda2\lVert w\rVert^2$ | $X^\top(Xw-y) + \lambda w = 0$ | $(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ |
| $\frac12\lVert\Phi w-y\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ (Fall 2025, Lecture 12) | $\Phi^\top(\Phi w-y) + 2\lambda w = 0$ | $(\Phi^\top\Phi + 2\lambda I)^{-1}\Phi^\top y$ |
| $\frac1N\lVert Xw-y\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ | $\frac2NX^\top(Xw-y) + 2\lambda w = 0$ | $(X^\top X + N\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ |

All four give the same *family* of solutions; only the meaning of a particular number $\lambda$ changes. (The Lecture 12 review writes the third objective with $\lambda I$ in the answer; with the $\frac12$ on the data term only, the derivation gives $2\lambda I$.)

> [!example] Ridge on three points
> Fit $y = mx + b$ to $(0,1), (1,2), (2,4)$ with $\lambda = 1$ (penalty on both $m$ and $b$, as in the lecture). From [[3-linear-models/linear-regression|linear regression]], $X^\top X = \begin{bmatrix}5&3\\3&3\end{bmatrix}$ and $X^\top y = \begin{bmatrix}10\\7\end{bmatrix}$. Then
> $$
> X^\top X + I = \begin{bmatrix}6&3\\3&4\end{bmatrix},\quad \det = 15,\quad
> w^* = \frac1{15}\begin{bmatrix}4&-3\\-3&6\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}10\\7\end{bmatrix} = \frac1{15}\begin{bmatrix}19\\12\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1.267\\0.8\end{bmatrix}.
> $$
> Plain least squares gave $(1.5,\ 0.833)$: the penalty shrank both weights.

Twenty points from a quadratic, four of them shifted up by 30, fit with $\Phi$ rows $[x_i^2,\ x_i,\ 1]$:

```python
# Lecture 8: least squares vs ridge
Phi = np.array([[e**(2-i) for i in range(3)] for e in x])

```

## Overfitting a high-degree polynomial (Lecture 8)

The data comes from $3x^2 + 2x + 1$ plus noise, but suppose you do not know the degree and fit a degree-10 polynomial (11 weights) to 20 points. Without regularization the notebook's fitted coefficients include $37.99$ and $-40.33$ on $x^6$ and $x^4$; with $\lambda = 10$ every coefficient is below $1.1$ in size and the curve follows the data.

```python
# Lecture 8: degree-10 fit, then ridge
D = 10
Phi = np.array([[e**(D-i) for i in range(D+1)] for e in x])   # (20, 11)
w = np.linalg.inv(Phi.T @ Phi + lam*np.eye(D+1)) @ Phi.T @ y
```

### Choosing $\lambda$ from a plot

| what the fitted curve does | diagnosis | change |
|---|---|---|
| swings far outside the data between points, spikes near the edges (Fall 2025 5c; HW3 2b) | overfitting | **increase** $\lambda$ |
| nearly flat, misses an obvious trend (HW3 2c) | underfitting | **decrease** $\lambda$ |
| smooth, follows the trend through the noise (HW3 2a) | about right | leave $\lambda$ |

In practice $\lambda$ is chosen by trying several values and comparing the error on held-out **validation** data, never on the training data (where $\lambda = 0$ always wins). See [[4-pytorch-training/experimental-setup|experimental setup]].

## Ridge by gradient descent: weight decay

With the gradient above, a gradient-descent step is
$$
w^{(k+1)} = w^{(k)} - 2\alpha\left(X^\top(Xw^{(k)} - y) + \lambda w^{(k)}\right) = (1 - 2\alpha\lambda)\,w^{(k)} - 2\alpha X^\top(Xw^{(k)} - y).
$$
Every step first multiplies the weights by $(1-2\alpha\lambda) < 1$, "decaying" them toward zero, then takes the usual least-squares step. PyTorch optimizers expose the same idea as the `weight_decay` argument of [`torch.optim.SGD`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.SGD.html), which adds $\text{wd}\cdot\theta$ to each parameter's gradient, the gradient of a penalty $\frac{\text{wd}}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert_2^2$. So the lecture's $\lambda\lVert w\rVert_2^2$ (gradient $2\lambda w$) corresponds to `weight_decay` $= 2\lambda$; see [[4-pytorch-training/optimizers|optimizers]].

```python
# closed-form ridge in PyTorch
d = X.shape[1]
w = torch.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + lam * torch.eye(d), X.T @ y)

# the same penalty through an optimizer
opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1e-4)
```

> [!trap] Common mistakes
> - **Dropping the factor of 2.** If only the data term has a $\frac12$, the answer has $2\lambda I$. Derive from the gradient.
> - **Thinking $\lambda$ is learned.** Minimizing the training loss over $\lambda$ would always pick $\lambda = 0$. It is a hyperparameter, tuned on validation data.
> - **"Larger $\lambda$ is always safer."** Too large a $\lambda$ shrinks everything toward 0 and underfits.
> - **The bias is penalized too here.** The lecture's $\lVert w\rVert^2$ includes $b$, which pulls the intercept toward 0 (visible in the three-point example). Many libraries leave the bias out of the penalty.
> - **Mixing up the identity's size.** $I$ is $d\times d$ (one entry per weight), not $N\times N$.

> [!exam] On the exams
> Fall 2025 Q5(c) showed a high-degree polynomial fit with $\lambda = 10$ that dives far below the data in the gap between points around $x = -1.5$: **increase $\lambda$**, it is overfitting. Expect to derive the ridge solution from its gradient with whatever constants the problem uses, and to explain why it is invertible when $d > N$. See [[6-midterm-1/fall-2025|Fall 2025]] and the [[6-midterm-1/formula-sheet|formula sheet]].
